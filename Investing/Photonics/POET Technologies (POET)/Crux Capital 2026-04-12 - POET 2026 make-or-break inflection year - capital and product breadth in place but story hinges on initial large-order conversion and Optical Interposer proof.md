## Key Takeaways

- [[POET Technologies (POET)]] is the rare sub-$1B name with direct exposure to the AI optical buildout, which is why it has become an X fan favorite. Crux treats it as a **smaller-weight, story-stock position** — most recent add at $5 — until execution catches up to the thesis. Bullish on the tech and TAM, but unwilling to size up before commercial conversion is proven.
- The thesis hinges on three 2026 deliverables: (1) **executing on the initial large order**, (2) **converting more customers into repeat programs**, and (3) showing the **Optical Interposer** actually delivers the manufacturing and integration benefits management has pitched for years. Until that sequence becomes clearer, POET stays smaller-weight rather than a core holding.
- Crux sees a real path to commercial inflection — POET now has capital, product breadth (Optical Interposer + transceiver designs), manufacturing setup, and customer activity in place for the first time. The ingredients are there; the question is execution, not architecture.
- POET sits in the photonic-interposer/CPO race alongside [[Marvell Technology (MRVL)]] (chiplet/co-packaging push) and [[Mitsubishi Electric (MIELY)]] (laser supply partnership), with private competition from Celestial AI, Lightmatter, and Ayar Labs.
- Asymmetric, binary setup: significant upside if POET executes, significant downside if it stumbles. Crux frames this explicitly — small position size matches the binary nature of the catalysts rather than averaging into a "core" weight.

> ## Executive Summary
>
> POET Technologies is a Canadian photonics company developing optical engines for high-speed data communications. Its core technology, the Optical Interposer, is designed to simplify how optical engines are assembled by eliminating active optical alignment, one of the hardest and most expensive steps in conventional optical packaging.
>
> That technology definitely appears credible. It has been demonstrated publicly at major industry events, received independent recognition from Lightwave, and has meaningful third-party commercial proof point through Adtran, which has independently shown a product using POET’s engines. POET also has also maintained a real multi-year relationship with Celestial AI, though the scope of any continuing role following Marvell’s acquisition of Celestial has not been made official.
>
> The harder question is commercial scale. POET’s reported revenue remains very small, and the company is still early in the transition from engineering work and sample shipments to repeatable product revenue. It has disclosed initial production orders, including a larger 800G Infinity order expected to ship in the second half of 2026.
>
> POET now has the capital to pursue its manufacturing ramp, product development, and customer programs, but the investment case still depends on a sequence that we still need to see proven: production starting on time, yields holding up at scale, initial orders converting into deliveries, and additional customers following.
>
> This report is built around a simple question: does POET’s technical differentiation translate into a real commercial position in optical interconnects, or does the gap between engineering promise and market proof remain too wide?
>

> ## What POET Is
>
> POET Technologies is trying to improve one of the hardest parts of optical hardware: the way the optical engine gets built.
>
> [![[cruxcapitalgroup-poet-tech-deep-dive-003.png]]](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!AiwT!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F897a736e-88c8-4a6a-830d-b9033ec73685_2414x1260.png)
>
> At the center of every high-speed optical transceiver is an optical engine. That is the part that brings together lasers, photodetectors, and waveguides so electrical signals can be converted into light, sent through fiber, and then turned back into electrical signals on the other side. Those components often come from different material systems and have to be assembled with extreme precision. In conventional optical packaging, that process could be slow, expensive, and hard to scale.
>
> [![[cruxcapitalgroup-poet-tech-deep-dive-004.png]]](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!Urfc!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F448e94ed-fd50-41b2-b741-67b8c1ce0e12_2490x1284.png)
>
> POET’s core idea is the Optical Interposer. POET describes it as a platform that uses lithographically defined alignment features to position optical components where they need to go, with the goal of replacing the labor-intensive alignment steps used in traditional optical assembly.
>
> [![[cruxcapitalgroup-poet-tech-deep-dive-005.jpeg]]](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!D0KW!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fedf90c01-145e-4027-b693-31cc2171f45f_651x258.jpeg)
>
> The commercial case for POET is really a manufacturing argument. The Optical Interposer is meant to push photonics toward wafer-scale assembly and testing, tighter integration, and a cost curve that improves with scale more like a chip than a custom-built optical module. Suresh Venkatesan described POET as pioneering a new wafer-scale packaging technology for the integration of photonics and electronics, and in a more recent interview Raju Kankipati said wafer-scale assembly and testing had already been achieved to a large extent, with further work underway to extend that across the product portfolio.
>
> [![[cruxcapitalgroup-poet-tech-deep-dive-006.png]]](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!ZxNm!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F182f43ab-f4b7-47a2-8aec-690d629fd96c_2414x1260.png)
>
> If that works, optical engines become easier to assemble, easier to test, easier to scale, and more flexible in how different optical components are combined. That is why POET gets attention. Optical interconnect demand is rising quickly as data center speeds move higher and AI infrastructure consumes more bandwidth. In that environment, the bottleneck is increasingly tied to the industry’s ability to build optics efficiently, repeatedly, and at scale. POET is trying to address that bottleneck at the production-model level rather than only at the component level.
>
> [![[cruxcapitalgroup-poet-tech-deep-dive-007.png]]](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!cZBW!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F2824f2b1-7e2a-4bbb-b921-91425399c328_1636x460.png)
>
> The leadership team adds credibility to that ambition. CEO Dr. Suresh Venkatesan came from GlobalFoundries, where he worked on advanced-node development. Chief Revenue Officer Raju Kankipati brings product and commercial experience from MACOM, Arista, and Cisco. That background fits the company’s attempt to bring semiconductor-style manufacturing thinking into optical hardware and then carry it into commercial production.

> ## Market Context and Tailwinds
>
> POET’s setup looks more relevant in the current market than it did even a year ago. I say this because optical interconnect demand is rising quickly, the upstream laser layer is under pressure, and large customers are paying closer attention to where components are built and how efficiently they can be manufactured. The industry backdrop is becoming more favorable to the type of problem POET is trying to solve.
>
> ### The EML Constraint
>
> One of the most important bottlenecks in AI optics now sits upstream, at the laser layer. NVIDIA made that unusually clear in March 2026 when it announced strategic partnerships with both Lumentum and Coherent, including large capital commitments and future access tied to advanced laser and optical networking capacity. The message is basically that the industry is now competing for the optical components required to build next-generation systems.
>
> [![[cruxcapitalgroup-poet-tech-deep-dive-008.png]]](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!3Ioh!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F09bf2c99-6fc4-4685-870f-a0bb33990038_1178x434.png)
>
> So where does POET fit into this? POET’s Teralight is built around Mitsubishi Electric’s EML technology. POET says the 1.6T transmit engine uses Mitsubishi’s “2x200G EML” design, allowing the engine to use four packaged EML chips instead of a more conventional eight-chip 1.6T approach. The key nuance is where that advantage sits. Public materials support a reduction in packaged chip count at the system level. What isn’t yet established is a one-for-one reduction in underlying laser content, cost, or total supply burden.
>
> [![[cruxcapitalgroup-poet-tech-deep-dive-009.jpeg]]](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!zee7!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F19e6aeaf-3a13-4a22-bcaa-25991771f88b_978x496.jpeg)
>
> So I think the most logical way to frame POET here is as a company pursuing greater efficiency inside a constrained part of the stack. In a tight EML market, fewer packaged chips per engine could be important. The commercial value of that design choice will be determined by production, yield, and customer adoption.
>
> ### **The AI Infrastructure Buildout**
>
> Demand is the other side of the picture. It is estimated that Alphabet, Amazon, Meta, and Microsoft alone could spend over $600 billion on AI infrastructure in 2026. At the same time projected shipments of 800G-and-higher optical transceivers rising from about 24 million units in 2025 to nearly 63 million in 2026.
>
> [![[cruxcapitalgroup-poet-tech-deep-dive-010.webp]]](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!IFKw!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fdddec607-5364-47ed-a25e-0bb90cad2683_894x490.webp)
>
> That backdrop is relevant for POET because scale raises the value of execution across the hardware stack. Grid access, power equipment, switches, packaging, lasers, testing, and optical assembly all remain important constraints. As optical volumes rise and speeds move higher, the manufacturing layer carries more weight within that broader system. Packaging precision, laser availability, process control, yield, and production throughput start to shape who can actually serve demand at scale.
>
> That is where POET is trying to carve out its role. If AI optics keeps moving from a high-performance growth market into mass-volume infrastructure, manufacturing leverage becomes increasingly valuable.

> ## Products and Technology
>
> POET’s product line is best understood as a ladder with each rung representing a different level of commercial maturity, and the distance between rungs mattering a great deal for investors trying to assess near-term revenue potential.
>
> Before walking through the products, one architectural point deserves clear explanation because it affects how the product lineup should be read. POET makes two distinct types of products for two distinct deployment scenarios.
>
> In conventional pluggable transceivers (the dominant form factor in today’s 800G market and an important path into 1.6T) the laser source is integrated inside the transceiver module. POET’s Teralight engine serves this market. It places the lasers inside the engine, packages everything together, and plugs into a standard port.
>
> In co-packaged optics (CPO), where optical components are integrated directly onto the switch package, the light source is often supplied separately from the main optical package. POET’s Starlight and Blazar products serve this market. They package the light source separately as an external source for CPO-style and related optical architectures.
>
> These are not competing products. They target different deployment architectures. POET is positioned across both parts of that transition.
>
> ### Starlight - External Light Source Engines
>
> POET’s Starlight family is built for external light source architectures, where the laser is supplied separately from the main optical package rather than embedded directly inside a pluggable transceiver. Some next-generation optical systems, especially those tied to CPO and chip-to-chip optical interconnects, rely on shared light sources outside the core package. Starlight is POET’s product aimed at that part of the stack.
>
> At OFC 2026, management drew a clearer distinction between the first and second generations of the product. Gen 1 was framed as the platform that proved the technology and the viability of the solution. Gen 2 was framed as the version designed for high-volume manufacturing. In the live demonstration, management described an external light source module with eight high-power lasers built inside the POET platform, running close to 20 dBm of output power per channel with tightly integrated wavelengths. That gives the product a more concrete shape.
>
> A next-generation Starlight packaged in the industry-standard ELSFP format shows that POET is building toward a form factor the broader ecosystem is trying to standardize.
>
> ### Infinity - 800G Optical Engines
>
> The Infinity engine family targets the 800G speed tier which is the level at which most leading AI data centers are currently deploying new optical links at volume. POET’s Infinity engine is a 400G component; two of them, one for transmitting and one for receiving, are combined inside a finished module for 800G total throughput.
>
> Final design samples were shipped to three global customers in March 2025. POET has received two production orders: the larger, over $5 million for Infinity 800G engines from an unnamed customer, filed in October 2025, with delivery expected in H2 2026; and a smaller order bringing total disclosed production orders to approximately $5.6 million. The unnamed customer is described in management’s April 2026 communication to a commissioned analyst (Zacks SCR, Lisa Thomsphon) as a “non-Chinese Asian” company with data center customers already lined up.
>
> Production of 800G engines at the Malaysia facility is targeted for Q3 2026. Management’s full-year 2026 target is to ship more than 30,000 optical engines across all product lines.
>
> ### Teralight - 1.6T Optical Engines
>
> The Teralight targets 1.6 terabits per second and was co-developed with Mitsubishi Electric using their 2×200G EML laser chips. As discussed in the market context section, the design’s use of four EML chips instead of the conventional eight carries additional significance given the current EML supply shortage. This is POET’s design claim as independent cost or yield validation at production volume does not exist.
>
> The Teralight has been demonstrated at OFC 2025, CIOE 2025, and OFC 2026. In February 2026, it received an Elite Score of 4.5 out of 5 from Lightwave Innovation Review. POET has won Lightwave Innovation Awards in two consecutive years. In January 2026, it also won a Product Innovation Award at China’s Infostone Awards. These are the strongest independent technical recognitions the product has received.
>
> In March 2026, POET and Lessenger, a South Korean module, announced an expanded partnership targeting 1.6T 2×DR4 transceiver samples in Q2 2026.
>
> ### **Blazar - High-Power Laser Platform**
>
> Blazar is POET’s high-power laser platform for ELS architectures tied to CPO and chip-to-chip optical links. At OFC 2026, management described it as an external cavity laser built on the Optical Interposer, with indium phosphide gain material and wavelength selection handled at the interposer level.
>
> The product story here centers on manufacturability and power. In the OFC 2026 demo, management said the design is intended to be easier to make and easier to yield than a traditional DFB-based approach, while also pointing to demonstrated output near 18 dBm and a goal of exceeding 20 dBm this year. Management has also suggested the architecture can improve the effective supply efficiency of indium phosphide by enabling more scalable packaging around scarce laser material. That remains a company claim rather than an independently validated materials-savings figure, but it adds another reason Blazar could matter if laser-related bottlenecks persist.
>
> The market opportunity is real and growing, with ELS becoming more relevant as optical engines move closer to the switch or accelerator package.
>
> ### **3.2T TFLN Development**
>
> In November 2025, POET and Quantum Computing Inc. announced a collaboration to develop a 400G-per-lane thin-film lithium niobate, or TFLN, modulator integrated with the POET Optical Interposer. A modulator is the component that encodes electrical data onto light, so this project sits at the core of the next speed transition in optical networking. At 400G per lane, an eight-lane design reaches 3.2 terabits per second, which places this program on the roadmap beyond today’s 800G and 1.6T products. Under the agreement, POET is funding the modulator development, with completion targeted for the second half of 2026.
>
> [![[cruxcapitalgroup-poet-tech-deep-dive-011.png]]](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!2ybI!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F620e298a-e0a1-4867-9341-5ae01c8ab970_1484x606.png)
>
> TFLN is drawing attention because it combines high bandwidth, low power consumption, lower insertion loss, and small device size, which makes it attractive for dense photonic integration. More broadly, TFLN manufacturing shows why the material is part of the 400G-per-lane conversation with data-center optics at that speed requiring modulators with bandwidth above 100 GHz, and TFLN offers a seemingly credible path toward that performance together with a potential wafer-level manufacturing roadmap.
>
> This program should still be treated as long-dated optionality rather than a near-term revenue driver. QCi is a small partner, the work remains in development, and the public record still lacks customer qualifications, volume plans, or commercial commitments. The significance of the announcement shows POET trying to build a seat at the table for the post-1.6T generation of optical engines.

> ## Business Model
>
> POET’s revenue model is straightforward in structure, though the commercial execution of it remains largely ahead of the company rather than behind it.
>
> ### How POET Makes Money
>
> POET sells optical engines (physical products) to the companies that build finished transceiver modules. Those module makers integrate POET’s engines into their designs and sell the completed modules into data center, AI network, and telecom markets. POET sits one step upstream from the end customer.
>
> Through the end of fiscal 2025, the vast majority of reported revenue came from non-recurring engineering fees (payments from customers for the development and integration work required to incorporate POET’s engines into their module designs) along with small sample shipments. NRE revenue is real income, but it is not the recurring product revenue.
>
> ### The Customer Structure
>
> POET serves three main customer lanes. The first is the AI data-center lane, where it sells into the companies building optical connectivity for AI clusters and hyperscale data centers. These are transceiver module makers, systems integrators, and related suppliers serving high-speed networking demand around 800G and 1.6T pluggables. This is the part of the business tied most directly to today’s AI-network buildout and the area where POET has already disclosed sample shipments and production orders.
>
> [![[cruxcapitalgroup-poet-tech-deep-dive-012.png]]](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!JDgG!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fa5bedf9c-97a9-467b-b1a6-dbbd955e56b4_1484x1130.png)
>
> The second lane is chip-to-chip and co-packaged optical interconnect. These customers are working on architectures that bring optics closer to the compute package and rely on external light sources as part of the system design. This is the segment POET is targeting with its external-light-source roadmap, which is aimed at next-generation AI systems where bandwidth density, power efficiency, and optical integration become even more important.
>
> The third lane is telecom and mobile front-haul. Here, POET is using the same platform technology in communications infrastructure outside the core AI data-center market. The collaboration with NTT Innovative Devices fits into this lane, with the two companies developing a 100G bidirectional optical engine for next-generation mobile front-haul networks.
>
> The distinction between these lanes matters because they sit at different points on the roadmap. The AI data-center lane is tied to the current 800G and 1.6T optical buildout. The chip-to-chip lane points toward a more advanced generation of AI interconnect architecture. The telecom lane extends the platform into a separate communications market with its own adoption cycle. That makes the first lane the most relevant for the near-term story, while the second and third provide additional upside if those programs deepen over time.
>
> ### Why Customers Would Stay
>
> Once a module maker integrates POET’s engine into their product design, switching to an alternative involves re-qualifying the module, which is a process that is typically lengthy in optical components. Management characterizes this switching cost as 18 months or more. POET’s interposer integrates passive alignment features specific to its platform, meaning a competing engine would require a new module design and not merely a component swap.
>
> ### Manufacturing Structure and Costs
>
> POET’s manufacturing model now spans both Malaysia and China. In Penang, it works with Globetronics and NationGate on manufacturing, assembly, and test under disclosed agreements. In China, it controls Super Photonics Xiamen, which became a wholly owned subsidiary at the end of 2024 after acquiring Sanan’s remaining stake.
>
> The Malaysia footprint carries more detail than a typical outsourced setup. In a CEO interview last year, Suresh Venkatesan described custom clean-room facilities housed inside the contract manufacturers, with POET-installed equipment alongside equipment purchased by the partners. He framed that buildout as the step that takes POET from prototype manufacturing toward the capacity to produce more than one million optical engines a year. In the same interview, he also described NationGate specifically as the module manufacturing partner for AI-hardware products.
>
> That structure shapes both cost and execution. A partner-based model gives POET a capital-light path into scale, so more of the balance sheet can go toward process development, customer programs, and working capital instead of a fully owned high-volume manufacturing footprint. At the same time, process transfer, coordination, and manufacturing yield carry more weight in the story. Yield performance, meaning the percentage of engines that meet specification, sits at the center of the unit-economics question.
>
> Management has also recently affirmed that the combined assembly and test footprint can exceed one million optical engines per year. Against that, the 2026 shipment target is more than 30,000 units, or only a small fraction of stated capacity. In the newer CRO interview, Raju Kankipati said a healthy number of units are scheduled to ship over the next three to four quarters and tied the near-term focus to qualification and production-line readiness. Those two datapoints frame the current stage of the ramp clearly: the task now is turning that footprint into reliable output, acceptable yields, and meaningful revenue.

> ## Partnerships and Ecosystem
>
> POET has announced partnerships with more than a dozen companies. Not all of them carry the same weight, and the difference between an independently corroborated relationship and one that rests entirely on a POET press release is material for investors assessing commercial traction.
>
> ### Adtran
>
> The Adtran relationship is important because it shows how POET’s technology fits into an actual product.
>
> POET supplies the optical engine for Adtran’s MicroMux Quattro, a pluggable product designed to let operators run four 100GbE links inside a single 400GbE QSFP-DD slot. The use case here is that it helps customers support legacy 100G services on newer 400G hardware without adding separate aggregation equipment. For POET, the revenue path here is simple as well where they supply the engine, have it integrated into the finished product, and participate one layer upstream from the end customer.
>
> The fit between the two companies also makes sense at the product level. MicroMux Quattro is a dense, highly integrated pluggable design, and POET’s pitch has always centered on compact optical engines and tighter optical packaging. That made Adtran a natural match for the kind of integration problem POET is trying to solve.
>
> ### Celestial AI and Marvell
>
> The Celestial AI relationship remains one of the most important pieces of POET’s partner set because it ties the company to a high-profile optical scale-up architecture rather than a standard module program. POET and Celestial were publicly linked over multiple years, and POET said in mid-2024 that its light-source technology was being used in Celestial’s Photonic Fabric platform. That made Celestial the clearest example of POET participating in the external-light-source side of the AI optical stack.

> ### Foxconn Interconnect Technology
>
> The FIT relationship was one of POET’s clearest early signals that its optical engines could find a place inside mainstream pluggable transceivers. In May 2024, POET said FIT had selected its engines for 800G and 1.6T pluggable modules and laid out a specific commercialization roadmap around that design win. For a company still trying to move from technology story to product revenue, that announcement carried real weight.
>
> The original roadmap was straightforward: engine design completion by Q3 2024, production at Super Photonics Xiamen by Q4 2024, customer sampling over the following six months, and first 800G volume production orders expected in the second half of 2025. That timeline gave investors a concrete sequence to watch rather than a vague long-term collaboration.
>
> What makes the relationship harder to judge today is that the later public record became less specific. POET has continued to discuss broader commercialization progress and has disclosed an over-$5 million Infinity 800G production order from an unnamed customer, but the company has not clearly said that order came from FIT. The description could possibly fit due to the timing, but there is not enough public evidence to make that attribution with confidence.
>
> At the same time, FIT’s own announcements show a company expanding across multiple optical and interconnect programs, including CPO-related work, external laser source platforms, and other high-speed connectivity efforts. This suggests that FIT should be viewed as part of a broader optical roadmap rather than as a single-thread relationship moving cleanly from the original 2024 announcement into confirmed revenue.
>
> FIT remains relevant because of the scale and ambition of the original design win. The open question is how much of that original opportunity is still sitting in front of POET today, and that is something the company still needs to clarify more directly.

> ### Luxshare
>
> In August 2024, POET said the partnership expanded after successful testing of an 800G 2xFR4 OSFP module using POET’s receive optical engines. Under the expanded scope, Luxshare would use POET’s transmit and receive engines in 400G and 800G single-mode pluggable transceivers, with Luxshare manufacturing and selling the finished modules globally.
>
> POET later said the relationship also extended into the 1.6T roadmap, with Luxshare among the partners tied to 1.6T transceivers targeted for sampling in late 2025 and production in late 2026. That makes Luxshare important because it gives POET a route into finished products across the current 400G/800G cycle and the early 1.6T transition.

> ### Mitsubishi Electric
>
> The Mitsubishi relationship sits directly underneath one of POET’s most important product claims. Mitsubishi supplies the EML technology used in Teralight, while POET supplies the engine architecture and packaging platform around it. So Mitsubishi provides the laser chip and POET provides the optical engine that can be integrated into a finished 1.6T transceiver.
>
> POET says Teralight uses Mitsubishi’s 2x200G EML design, which is the basis for its claim that the 1.6T transmit engine can use four packaged EML chips instead of a more conventional eight-chip setup. That is what makes this relationship strategically important. This is a product-specific collaboration tied to the laser layer of the stack, which is also one of the most constrained and expensive parts of advanced optical systems.
>
> The output of the collaboration is already visible. Teralight has been demonstrated at OFC 2025, CIOE 2025, and OFC 2026, and it received an Elite Score of 4.5 out of 5 from Lightwave Innovation Reviews in February 2026. The open questions sit further downstream, around customer adoption, manufacturing readiness, and whether the design advantage turns into a real commercial advantage at scale. But the role of each side is clear: Mitsubishi supplies the laser technology, POET packages it into an optical engine, and the revenue opportunity depends on that engine being designed into future 1.6T modules.

> ### Lessengers and LITEON
>
> Lessengers and LITEON sit at different points on POET’s roadmap, but both show how POET is trying to move from supplying optical engines as a component to becoming part of a finished module platform.
>
> The Lessengers relationship is the more immediate of the two. In March 2026, POET and Lessengers said they were jointly developing a 1.6T 2×DR4 optical transceiver module for AI clusters and hyperscale data center networks. POET contributes the optical engine built on its Optical Interposer platform, while Lessengers contributes its Direct Optical Wiring, or DOW, technology, which is meant to simplify optical routing between photonic devices and fiber interfaces. POET’s role is to supply the optical engine. Lessengers’ role is to build the finished module around it. Samples were targeted for Q2 2026. The market they are aiming at is also large enough to matter. In the same announcement, POET cited a LightCounting forecast for more than 125 million 1.6T DR8 units from 2027 through 2031 and noted that 2×DR4 serves that same market with a different connector format. That gives the Lessengers effort a clearer revenue context than a typical early-stage module announcement.
>
> LITEON is earlier, but broader in strategic significance. In March 2026, POET and LITEON announced a joint development effort to build next-generation optical communication modules for AI applications. POET said the module would be built on its Optical Interposer platform, while LITEON would bring optoelectronic and manufacturing expertise. The stated design uses the Optical Interposer to integrate lasers, optics, drive electronics, and coupling structures into a compact, thermally optimized module aimed at co-packaged optics, AI systems, and high-bandwidth data center applications. POET supplies the integration platform and engine architecture; LITEON supplies the module-development and production capability needed to bring that product to market. Development is expected to begin in 2026, prototypes are targeted for late 2026, and high-volume production is anticipated for 2027.
>
> ### Sivers Semiconductors
>
> The Sivers relationship fits on the external-light-source side of POET’s strategy. In November 2025, POET and Sivers said they were working together on light engines for AI infrastructure, combining POET’s Optical Interposer platform with Sivers’ laser technology. The commercial logic is straightforward where Sivers contributes the laser side, POET contributes the integration platform, and the value sits in building a more complete light-engine solution for next-generation optical links.
>
> This relationship shows POET trying to sit closer to the light-engine layer of the stack rather than only the finished transceiver module.
>
> ### Semtech
>
> In September 2025, POET and Semtech introduced a 1.6T receive optical engine, combining POET’s Optical Interposer-based receiver with Semtech’s direct-drive linear TIAs. POET provides the receiver engine, Semtech provides the analog front-end electronics, and the combined product is aimed at 1.6T optical modules for AI networks.
>
> This shows how POET can fit into a more standard module roadmap. POET is not trying to build the whole transceiver itself. It is supplying the optical engine portion and pairing it with a complementary component supplier so the finished module maker has a more complete solution to design around. Like the Sivers relationship, this one does not come with disclosed contract values or revenue timing, but the commercial path is easier to understand because it sits closer to the current 1.6T module buildout.

> ### Quantum Computing Inc.
>
> The QCI relationship sits further out on POET’s roadmap than most of its other partnerships. In November 2025, POET and Quantum Computing Inc. said they would work together to develop a 3.2T optical engine built around 400G-per-lane thin-film lithium niobate, or TFLN, modulators. POET’s role is to provide the Optical Interposer platform. QCI’s role is to provide the TFLN modulator technology. The target is a higher-speed engine for future optical links rather than a near-term production product.
>
> The commercial structure is different from POET’s module partnerships. This is a development program, with POET funding the modulator work and QCI supplying the underlying device expertise. In other words, POET is not selling an engine into QCI here. POET is investing alongside a modulator partner to build a more advanced future product.
>
> This expands POET’s roadmap into 3.2T and beyond, and it shows the company trying to position the Optical Interposer for next-generation engine designs rather than only today’s 800G and 1.6T cycle. The significance is strategic and technical first. The commercial value sits further down the road.
>
> ### Globetronics and NationGate
>
> Globetronics and NationGate sit at the center of POET’s manufacturing ramp in Penang. Globetronics was the first major step, with agreements covering optical engine production. NationGate followed in June 2025, extending the buildout into optical engine assembly and test, including work tied to a custom fiber-attach unit for one of POET’s key customers. Together, they form the backbone of POET’s Malaysia production strategy.
>
> The role of each partner has become clearer over time. In the CEO interview fron last year, Suresh Venkatesan described custom clean-room facilities housed inside the contract manufacturers, with POET-installed equipment alongside equipment purchased by the partners. He also described NationGate specifically as the module manufacturing partner for products in the AI hardware segment. That makes the Penang footprint feel like a purpose-built production ecosystem built around POET’s process.
>
> This is where POET is trying to move from development-stage output into repeatable manufacturing flow. By mid-2025, POET said Globetronics had progressed far enough to ship its backlog of 800G sample optical engines to existing customers, with 1.6T samples expected next. In the newer CRO interview, Raju Kankipati said a healthy number of units are scheduled to ship over the next three to four quarters, with qualification and production-line readiness as the near-term focus. The real value of both partnerships sits in execution: process transfer, manufacturing readiness, and the ability to support customer programs with real output.

> ### NTT Innovative Devices
>
> The NTT Innovative Devices relationship takes POET into a different part of the optical market. In August 2025, POET said the two companies were working together on a 100G bidirectional optical engine for next-generation mobile front-haul networks. POET contributes the Optical Interposer platform and packaging approach, while NTT Innovative Devices contributes the optical component side.
>
> That makes the relationship strategically useful because it broadens POET beyond AI transceivers and external light sources into telecom-oriented optical engines. The near-term revenue impact is unclear, but the partnership shows the platform being positioned for another optical end market with a large incumbent partner.

> ## Competitive Landscape
>
> POET is competing in three different ways at once, and each one matters for a different reason.
>
> The first layer is the incumbent pluggables market. Companies such as Innolight, Eoptolink, Coherent, Lumentum, Accelink, and AAOI already ship at scale into 800G and the early 1.6T transition. They compete through established hyperscaler relationships, mature supply chains, and the ability to deliver volume on time. That is the main challenge for POET on the high-speed engine side: it is entering a market where the leading players already know how to manufacture and qualify products at very large scale.
>
> The second layer is architecture. POET is not only competing against other suppliers of optical parts. It is also competing against more integrated ways of building the optical stack. TSMC’s COUPE platform is the clearest example. COUPE is expected to enter volume production in 2026, which means silicon photonics and CPO are gaining a foundry-scale manufacturing path. Broadcom is pushing the same direction from the system side, tying optical interconnects more tightly to switching silicon and highlighting a mature CPO partner ecosystem built around laser source cages, connectors, and optical packaging. These platforms compete with POET by trying to solve the packaging and integration problem inside a deeper, more native stack.
>
> The third layer is the startup field. Ayar Labs and Lightmatter are trying to move optics deeper into future chip-to-chip and scale-up architectures, while Teramount is attacking the packaging problem more directly with self-aligning optical connectivity and fiber-to-chip coupling. Teramount is especially relevant because it is also focused on easing photonic assembly and alignment, which means it competes with POET closer to the manufacturing layer than the module layer.
>
> That is where POET’s relevance has to come from. POET is not trying to outscale Innolight or Coherent in finished transceivers, and it is not trying to become Broadcom or TSMC. Its role is narrower. The bet is that the Optical Interposer can become a useful packaging and integration layer between the optical components and the finished module or light-source system. Basically POET is trying to win the step where lasers, optics, electronics, and coupling structures get assembled into something manufacturable. That is why the story leans so heavily on passive alignment, wafer-scale assembly, and partner-based production.
>
> So the real question is whether its packaging approach solves a manufacturing and integration problem well enough to stay relevant inside a market that is already crowded today and likely to become more integrated over time. The current window exists because pluggables still dominate and external light sources are becoming more important. The pressure comes from the fact that larger incumbents and better-funded platforms are also trying to define what the next optical stack looks like.

> ## Commercial Progress
>
> POET’s commercial progress now has a visible sequence. Adtran remains the clearest in-market example, with POET’s optical engines tied to the MicroMux Quattro platform, while Celestial AI carried Starlight into one of the more visible external-light-source architectures in the market. In March 2025, POET also shipped final design samples of its Infinity 800G transmit engines to three global customers, extending the story from product development into customer qualification.
>
> The next step showed up in the order book. POET disclosed two production orders totaling about $5.6 million, including an order of more than $5 million for Infinity 800G optical engines expected to ship in the second half of 2026. That order is significant because it takes the story from sample activity into committed demand tied to future delivery.
>
> The product sequence is also clearer now. In the March 2026 CRO interview, Raju Kankipati said a healthy number of units are scheduled to ship over the next three to four quarters, with near-term focus on qualification and production-line readiness. He specifically pointed to 100G LR4 engines for Adtran, 800G transmit and receive engines for DR8 and 2xFR4 transceivers, and external light-source products as the products about to enter production, with 1.6T Teralight engines following after that.
>
> That lines up with the operating milestones management laid out in the Q4 2025 results: light-source production in Malaysia targeted for Q2 2026, high-speed 800G optical engine production targeted for Q3, and more than 30,000 optical engines expected to ship during 2026 across high-speed and high-power segments. Previously announced module-development partnerships were also expected to begin contributing revenue during the year.
>
> Management’s framing has become more direct as the year has progressed. In the older CEO interview, Suresh Venkatesan described the Penang buildout as the step that takes POET from prototype manufacturing into the capacity to produce over a million optical engines a year. In the newer CRO interview, Kankipati said 2026 is the year to productize, enter production, and begin generating meaningful recurring revenue, while also pointing to several critical engagements with tier-one and tier-two customers and additional larger development programs that could form the base for 2027 and beyond.
>
> A sponsored April 2026 Zacks note added one more layer to that picture, describing expected 2026 shipments tied to Adtran, Celestial AI / Marvell, and an unnamed Asian customer. I think that is worth noting as current management-guided communication, but not treating as independent confirmation of customer scope or shipment timing.
>
> That is the right way to think about where the business stands. Product activity is already visible, purchase orders are already in hand, and the manufacturing transition is supposed to happen across 2026. The next phase is converting that sequence into shipped product, broader customer adoption, and recurring revenue.

> ## Financials
>
> POET’s financial profile comes down to three things: a very small current revenue base, a large cash cushion, and a valuation that already assumes the business can scale.
>
> Fiscal 2025 revenue was $1.07 million, with Q4 revenue of $341,202. Full-year net loss was $63.0 million, with roughly $35 million tied to non-cash items. Research and development expense was $18.1 million, while selling, marketing, and administrative expense was $25.1 million.
>
> The balance sheet is what gives POET time. Audited cash, equivalents, and short-term investments were $313.4 million at December 31, 2025. Management later said the January 2026 financing lifted that figure to roughly $430 million. At recent burn levels, that is a long runway even with higher spending tied to hiring, manufacturing scale-up, and customer programs. The financing question has largely been addressed. The work now sits in execution.
>
> The cost of that runway is dilution. Share count rose from roughly 99 million to about 152.7 million in a year, with another 37.4 million warrants still outstanding. That leaves POET with a much larger equity base than older narratives assumed. At roughly a billion-dollar market value, investors are already paying for a real commercialization path rather than a cash shell with optionality.
>
> ### Scenario Framework
>
> These are valuation scenarios anchored on FY2028 revenue. Markets can begin discounting those outcomes earlier as 2026 and 2027 execution improves visibility.
>
> **Bear case**
>
> POET reaches roughly **$50 million** of 2028 revenue.
>
> That would mean the first order ships, but the broader ramp stays slow. Follow-on demand builds gradually, 1.6T remains earlier-stage, and the partner set still looks more developmental than commercial. In that setup, I think roughly **4.0x sales** is fair, which points me to a stock value of about **$3 per share**.
>
> **Base case**
>
> POET reaches roughly **$175 million** of 2028 revenue.
>
> That would mean the initial order ships on time, Malaysia ramps well enough to support a visible 2027 step-up, and one or two additional customer programs begin to look commercially real. In that setup, I think roughly **6.5x sales** is fair, which points me to a stock value of about **$8.5 to $9 per share**.
>
> **Bull case**
>
> POET reaches roughly **$300 million** of 2028 revenue.
>
> That would mean several things go right at once: follow-on orders arrive, 1.6T starts converting into real module demand, and one of the larger open commercial threads such as Marvell/Celestial or another major customer resolves constructively. In that setup, I think roughly **8.0x sales** is fair, which points me to a stock value in the **mid-teens**, or about **$15 to $16 per share**.
>
> The key takeaway is where the stock already sits. Today’s valuation is much closer to a real commercialization outcome than to a pure optionality story. The balance sheet gives POET time to pursue that outcome. The market is still waiting for shipped product, follow-on demand, and a believable 2027–2028 revenue curve before paying materially more.

> ## What We Need to Watch For / Get Clarity On
>
> The next phase of the POET story is fairly easy to define. A small number of questions will do far more to shape the investment case than another conference appearance or another partnership headline.
>
> **1. Does the $5.6 million of disclosed order activity turn into shipped revenue?**  
> The most important near-term marker is the larger Infinity 800G order expected to ship in the second half of 2026. In the March 2026 CRO interview, Raju Kankipati said existing customers have already placed purchase orders and described fulfilling those orders during 2026 as the top priority. That is the clearest bridge between product qualification and commercial delivery.
>
> **2. Does the Malaysia ramp line up with the timetable management has laid out?**  
> Management has said light-source production should begin in Q2 2026, followed by 800G optical engine production in Q3, with more than 30,000 optical engines expected to ship during 2026. In the same March 2026 interview, Kankipati said a healthy number of units are scheduled to ship over the next three to four quarters and pointed to qualification and production-line readiness as the near-term focus. Those milestones now sit at the center of the story.
>
> **3. What do ASP and gross margin actually look like once volume starts moving?**  
> POET sells the optical engine rather than the full transceiver module, so the revenue opportunity sits at the engine layer of the stack. The two disclosed production orders and the 30,000-engine shipment target suggest realized pricing likely sits in the low hundreds of dollars per engine, with product mix doing a lot of the work. The bigger question is how much of the module economics POET captures as volume ramps. That answer will matter just as much as shipment growth.
>
> **4. Do new customers become visible, or does demand stay concentrated in a small set of programs?**  
> Management has been more specific on this point. In March 2026, Kankipati said POET was working on additional customer engagements and partnerships expected to convert into committed development programs over the next few months, including companies “even bigger than the ones we have announced before.” What investors need now is evidence of breadth: follow-on orders, additional named customers, or production commitments tied to real programs.
>
> **5. What is the current commercial scope of the Marvell/Celestial and FIT relationships?**  
> These remain two of the most important open threads in the story. Celestial stays strategically relevant because it ties POET to a high-profile optical scale-up architecture now sitting inside Marvell. FIT stays important because the original 2024 design win came with a clear commercialization roadmap. Both relationships still matter. What investors need is a clearer view of what each one means in present-day commercial terms.
>
> **6. How quickly does the 1.6T roadmap move from product narrative to customer-backed demand?**  
> Teralight, Lessengers, and LITEON all point toward the same next step: moving beyond 800G into a part of the market where POET’s architecture is supposed to matter even more. In the March 2026 interview, Kankipati said the products about to enter production include 800G engines and external light-source products, with 1.6T Teralight engines following after that. The signal investors need is straightforward in customer sampling that leads into design wins, production intent, and eventually revenue-bearing programs.
>
> **7. How far does wafer-scale assembly and testing extend across the product portfolio?**  
> Management has repeatedly framed wafer-scale assembly and testing as central to the long-term model. In March 2026, Kankipati said POET had achieved that to a large extent already and was continuing to invest so it could reach wafer-scale assembly across all product lines. That matters because the commercial case is not only about demand. It is also about whether the manufacturing model scales the way POET believes it can.
>
> That is really the watchlist. Orders, production, economics, customer breadth, relationship clarity, the 1.6T transition, and the reach of the wafer-scale manufacturing model will tell investors far more over the next few quarters than any single partnership announcement on its own.

> ## What Risks Exist
>
> **1. Manufacturing and yield risk**  
> POET’s architecture still has to prove itself in volume production. The commercial case depends on consistent output, acceptable yields, and smooth process transfer across the Malaysia manufacturing footprint. That is the core operational risk in the story.
>
> **2. Customer concentration risk**  
> Near-term revenue is concentrated in a very small number of programs and largely unnamed buyers. That leaves the commercial ramp sensitive to delays, design changes, or purchasing decisions at just a handful of customers.
>
> **3. Execution and timeline risk**  
> The next stage of the story depends on a specific sequence: production beginning on time, orders shipping, follow-on demand appearing, and 1.6T programs moving forward. Any slip in that sequence pushes the revenue curve out and weakens confidence in the broader roadmap.
>
> **4. Valuation risk**  
> The stock is already valued on the assumption that POET moves from development-stage revenue into a meaningful commercial ramp. That leaves less room for delays or partial execution than the small current revenue base might suggest.
>
> **5. Partner-visibility risk**  
> Two of the most important relationships in the story still carry open questions. Marvell/Celestial remains strategically important, but the current scope of POET’s supplier role is still not clearly defined. FIT remains relevant because of the scale of the original design win, but the present commercial path is still not fully clear.
>
> **6. Governance and promotion risk**  
> POET has used paid investor-outreach and marketing arrangements, including a 2022 agreement with Stockhouse / The Market Herald and an April 2024 expansion of outreach through Hybrid Financial and LFG. The remaining issue is how investors should weigh that promotional activity alongside the company’s operating story and forward-looking commercial claims, especially because outside critics later pointed to several campaigns as a factor in the stock’s trading pattern.
>
> **7. Competitive risk**  
> POET is pursuing markets already served by larger optical suppliers with established customer relationships, deeper manufacturing scale, and broader product portfolios. The architecture may be differentiated, but it still has to win its place inside an intensely competitive stack.
>
> **8. CPO timing risk**  
> Blazar and the external-light-source side of the story sit closest to a part of the market that still looks earlier in adoption. That keeps some of POET’s longer-dated upside tied to timing that remains outside its control.
>
> POET’s technology story is real, but the path from product promise to scaled revenue still runs through a small number of operational, commercial, and execution hurdles.
