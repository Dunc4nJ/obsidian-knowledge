### LPKF Laser & Electronics

The company is **LPKF Laser & Electronics**. It trades in Germany under **LPK**. There is also an OTC ticker, **LPKFF**, but the German listing is where I positioned as the OTC version can be extremely illiquid with wide spreads.

![[cruxcapitalgroup-new-position-high-upside-optionality-002.png]]

I took a starter position. That sizing is important because this is a small, volatile, under-earning European equipment company that has already moved sharply as investors started connecting it to glass-based advanced packaging.

I am buying the option. I am sizing it like an option. I want to be really clear here. If we get a **50% drawdown,** I wouldn't be surprised. This market is extremely sentiment & narrative driven right now. I have lots of exposure to large companies and I want to balance that out a bit with smaller, high optionality.

The basic thesis is simple though. LPKF is a German laser-equipment company with weak current financials, but one potentially valuable asset: **LIDE**, its laser-induced deep etching process for structuring glass with extreme precision. If glass-core substrates become a bigger part of AI and high-performance computing packaging, LPKF could sit near a key manufacturing bottleneck.

### What LPKF Actually Does

LPKF is much broader than the glass packaging story. The company has four main operating buckets: Development, Electronics, Welding, and Solar.

The **Development** segment is the prototyping business. LPKF sells rapid PCB prototyping systems into universities, engineering labs, corporate R&D groups, and research customers.

![[cruxcapitalgroup-new-position-high-upside-optionality-003.png]]

This is one of the steadier parts of the company. It is a practical engineering-tools business for customers that want in-house prototyping capability instead of sending every early design outside. Useful business, but far from the reason I bought the stock.

**Electronics** is the most important segment for the investment case.

![[cruxcapitalgroup-new-position-high-upside-optionality-004.png]]

This segment includes **PCB depaneling,** **SMT-related tools**, **legacy laser direct structuring exposure**, and the **advanced packaging / LIDE business**. PCB depaneling is a real niche.

![[cruxcapitalgroup-new-position-high-upside-optionality-005.png]]

LPKF sells laser systems that cut or separate printed circuit boards without the stress, dust, and mechanical damage that can come from more traditional methods. But the key piece inside Electronics is LIDE. That is the asset that has turned LPKF from a forgotten industrial small-cap into a name suddenly getting attention from AI supply-chain investors. More on LIDE later.

LPKF also sells laser **plastic welding systems.**

![[cruxcapitalgroup-new-position-high-upside-optionality-006.png]]

Historically, this business was tied heavily to automotive. That has been a problem because automotive has been weak, especially in Europe. Management is now trying to shift this business toward consumer electronics, medical, robotics, and new use cases like absorbing-to-absorbing plastic welding. There is some real technology here, but this is a repositioning story.

LPKF is also a leader in **thin-film solar laser scribing**. This business was historically meaningful and profitable, but it is currently in a weak phase. The issue is timing. Customers appear to be waiting for the next technology step, especially perovskite-related investment. That has created a pause in large solar equipment orders. Management still believes the perovskite transition can create future opportunity, but for now Solar is a drag.

That is the bulk of LPKF. A mix of useful niche laser businesses, a weak solar cycle, a restructuring program, and one potentially company-changing advanced packaging asset. Most of the company explains why the stock was ignored. One part of the company explains why investors are suddenly paying attention.

### The Current Numbers Are Messy

This is the part that keeps me from sizing aggressively.

LPKF generated €115.3 million of revenue in 2025. That was down 6.2% from the prior year. Adjusted EBIT was only €0.8 million. Order intake was €91.6 million, down from €114.3 million in 2024. Backlog fell to €27.1 million, down from €50.9 million at the end of 2024.

2026 guidance still points to a transition year. Management expects revenue of €105 million to €120 million and an adjusted EBIT margin between -3.0% and +4.5%. The company also expects restructuring costs of roughly 3% to 4% of revenue tied to North Star.

That is a rough operating setup. The company is in the middle of its North Star restructuring program. Management is cutting costs, simplifying the portfolio, and trying to build a business that can handle more volatility. The target is a sustainable double-digit EBIT margin by 2028.

The other important detail is that management's 2026 guidance excludes potential high-volume advanced-packaging orders. That means the official forecast still reflects the weak core business and restructuring phase, while the LIDE upside sits outside the base guide. That is exactly why this can look ugly on current numbers while still carrying meaningful optionality.

That target sounds attractive, but the market will need proof. LPKF has had promising technology stories before that took longer than expected to show up in the numbers. That is a real part of the risk.

So I want to be clear about the setup. This is very different from Smartoptics.

### Why Glass Is Entering The Conversation

The reason LPKF has become interesting is glass.

![[cruxcapitalgroup-new-position-high-upside-optionality-008.png]]

AI chips are getting larger and more complex. More HBM. More chiplets. Larger packages. More routing. More signal-integrity pressure. More warpage problems. More pressure on power delivery.

This is pushing the semiconductor industry to look harder at glass as a next-generation packaging material. For a long time, organic substrates have carried much of this workload. But as packages get larger and more complex, organic substrates can run into issues around warpage, dimensional stability, line spacing, signal integrity, and scaling.

![[cruxcapitalgroup-new-position-high-upside-optionality-009.png]]

Glass has attractive properties for advanced packaging. It can be very flat. It can offer strong dimensional stability. It can support larger panel formats. It may help with signal integrity at high speeds. It can potentially support the kind of routing density needed for larger AI and HPC packages.

![[cruxcapitalgroup-new-position-high-upside-optionality-010.png]]

This is why companies like Intel, Samsung, TSMC, Absolics, OSATs, and advanced packaging players are all being tied to glass substrate development, pilot lines, or roadmap work. The theme is real but the exact commercial timing is the unknown.

---

### The Bottleneck: Glass Is Hard To Process

Glass sounds attractive until you try to manufacture with it at semiconductor scale. The hard part is processing it cleanly, repeatedly, and economically.

You need to form tiny structures in glass. You need to create through-glass vias, which are vertical pathways through the glass. You need to do that without cracks, chips, hidden damage, or yield loss.

A material can have great theoretical properties and still fail commercially if the process economics break. That is the bottleneck.

Glass can be attractive as a packaging material, but the industry still needs ways to process it at production scale. LPKF's core argument is that LIDE solves one of the most important steps in that flow.

### What LIDE Does

LIDE stands for **Laser-Induced Deep Etching**. The basic idea is simple enough. LPKF uses a laser to modify a precise path inside the glass. Then chemistry etches the modified path much faster than the surrounding glass. That allows LPKF to create precise structures inside glass.

![[cruxcapitalgroup-new-position-high-upside-optionality-011.png]]

The key applications include through-glass vias, cavities, channels, glass interposer structures, glass-core substrate features, and precision microstructures for advanced packaging.

![[cruxcapitalgroup-new-position-high-upside-optionality-012.png]]

This is much more than using a laser to drill a hole. The value is in the process. The laser modification, the chemistry, the handling, the recipe, the throughput, the defect control, and the ability to repeat the process across wafers or panels.

That is why LIDE is interesting. LPKF may have one of the more credible ways to structure glass at scale while protecting the yield case.

![[cruxcapitalgroup-new-position-high-upside-optionality-013.png]]

The other important point is that LPKF is trying to broaden the platform. LIDE is the centerpiece, but management is building around it with Vitrion foundry services, NEXAR tool platforms, Tensor Ablation, Tensor Bonding, ABF singulation opportunities, and possible future co-packaged optics applications.

This is where the story becomes more interesting. The best version of the thesis is far more than one tool sale. The better version is that LPKF becomes a strategic process supplier for glass-based advanced packaging.

### What Management Is Saying

Management's language is the main reason I took this seriously. There are four points that stood out.

The first is the 80% qualification claim. Management said that more than **80% of customers preparing for glass and advanced packaging ramp-ups are qualifying with LPKF equipment**.

This is the key sentence. It means LPKF appears deeply embedded in the qualification funnel. Qualification is early. Production revenue comes later. But qualification is also where process choices start to form. If LPKF equipment is already being used across a large portion of relevant customer qualification work, that gives the company a seat at the table before the ramp begins.

That is exactly where I want to find small supply-chain companies: before the financials fully show it, before every institution has built the model, and before the story is fully obvious.

The second major point is the low triple-digit revenue potential. On the earnings call, an analyst asked whether management still expects LIDE to potentially lead to low triple-digit sales. The CEO said yes.

That is meaningful because LPKF's entire 2025 revenue base was only €115.3 million. If LIDE and adjacent advanced packaging tools eventually become a low triple-digit million euro revenue stream, that could rival the current scale of the entire company.

This is the optionality. A company doing roughly €100 million to €120 million in annual revenue has a very different profile if a new process platform adds tens of millions, then potentially more, at attractive margins. And then you layer on the bottleneck framing that the market loves.

The third key point is timing. Management's rough path is 2026 for first production starts and moderate production-related investments, 2027–2028 for the ramp phase, and 2029–2030 for the high-volume phase.

That timeline tells me two things. First, the stock can move before the revenue arrives because markets discount future inflections. Second, the stock can also sell off violently if investors expect too much too quickly.

The fourth point is what the first ramp may actually look like. Management is not talking about one giant purchase order for 100 machines. The CEO described the next step as a handful of customers buying a higher number of tools, still single-digit machines per customer, to move into true production flows and work yields toward the right level.

That is exactly how I want to frame 2026. Early production investment

### The Adjacent Pieces

The most important part of the thesis is LIDE, but the adjacent pieces make the story more interesting.

Glass packaging will need more than one process step.

Creating through-glass vias is critical, but customers also need to build the rest of the package around that glass. That includes removing material, opening layers, cutting or singulating package materials cleanly, bonding glass layers, inspecting the process, and eventually integrating glass into more advanced architectures.

That is why I care about the tools and services LPKF is building around LIDE.

**Vitrion** is LPKF's glass-processing foundry service. This gives customers a way to prototype and validate glass components before committing to their own production flow. That is useful because glass packaging is still early. Customers need process learning before they scale.

**NEXAR** is the equipment platform LPKF is building for glass substrate manufacturing. LPKF is trying to move from development work into production tools.

**Tensor Ablation** is aimed at removing or patterning material around the glass package flow. So once layers are built on or around the glass, customers still need to open, clean, cut, or shape parts of that stack with precision. That can become another process step where LPKF participates.

**Tensor Bonding** is aimed at joining glass layers together. Management framed this as one generation later in the architecture, so I would treat it as future optionality rather than a near-term driver.

The most interesting adjacent piece right now may be **ABF singulation** because management described it as customer pull. Customers came to LPKF with a pain point and asked whether LPKF could solve it. Management said ABF depaneling or singulation sits in the same production chain as LIDE, likely with a slight delay.

That makes it a potential attach opportunity.

The bull case gets stronger if LPKF becomes more than a one-step tool supplier. A customer that qualifies LIDE may eventually need adjacent tools for other parts of the glass packaging flow. If LPKF can attach those tools, the revenue opportunity expands.

Then there is **co-packaged optics** (CPO)

This is the later-stage option. When optical interconnects move closer to the chip package, glass could become relevant in future architectures for routing, interconnect, or integration. LPKF is participating in those conversations, but management made clear that architectures remain unsettled.

CPO is interesting, but it is far from the near-term thesis. The near-term watch item is glass-core substrates and whether LIDE moves from qualification into production. The adjacent tools make the upside larger if that transition happens.

LPKF does have real technology and qualification claims. It does sit near an important industry theme. But production share has yet to be proven. Major customers are mostly unnamed. The near-term financials are still weak. The stock has already caught momentum.

### Confirmed Ecosystem Signals

Intel has publicly discussed glass core substrates for advanced packaging later this decade. Absolics and SKC have invested in glass substrate capability. OSATs and packaging players are working on glass substrate and through-glass-via development. Fraunhofer launched a Glass Panel Technology Group, with LPKF contributing LIDE as a technology provider.

Onto Innovation's Packaging Applications Center of Excellence is focused on panel-level packaging, glass-core substrates, process control, inspection, and metrology. LPKF and Onto have also announced work around accelerating mass production of glass-core substrates, including metrology integration at LPKF's Vitrion facility.

That last point is important. Glass processing does not succeed through one tool alone. It needs metrology, inspection, process control, bonding, ablation, metallization, RDL, and yield learning. LPKF's position looks more credible when it appears inside a broader ecosystem trying to solve the full process flow.

That is why I care about the Onto and Fraunhofer connections. They help validate that LPKF is working near the right problem.

### Competition And Moat

The bullish story can sound too clean if we ignore competition.

LPKF has advantages. It has LIDE patents, process know-how, Vitrion, early qualification positioning, and years of work on glass before the theme became popular. Its patent position has also been strengthened by European and Korean protection.

That said, glass processing will not be a single-source market. Management has basically said this themselves. Customers want alternatives. Semiconductor customers especially want second sources. Asian competitors can be aggressive on price. China also has local-for-local pressure, which can hurt a German equipment supplier even if its technology is strong.

There are also other approaches to glass structuring and through-glass-via formation. Alternative routes include direct laser ablation, DRIE, photosensitive glass, plasma processes, and other glass-processing methods. There are also companies like Schmid, Philoptics, glass makers, laser tool vendors, and regional competitors participating in this market.

So LPKF may have a leading, protected, production-oriented process route for precision glass structuring, plus early customer qualification and process experience.

### Why This Could Be Large

The reason this is interesting is the size of the option compared to the size of the company.

LPKF is roughly a €100 million to €120 million revenue company today. Advanced packaging is still small inside reported numbers. But management has talked about low triple-digit million euro potential from LIDE and adjacent initiatives over time.

If that happens, it could change the whole company.

This is what I like in supply-chain investing. A small company with a very specific technology in a large industry transition with a revenue base small enough that even a partial win can move the needle.

The upside case does not require LPKF to become a giant. It requires LPKF to move from qualification tools and early production systems into meaningful production-related equipment and process revenue.

If LIDE becomes a real process step for glass-core substrates, and if LPKF attaches ablation, bonding, or related tools around that step, the revenue opportunity becomes much larger.

Then the cost structure matters. North Star is supposed to reduce the break-even point and push the company toward double-digit EBIT margins by 2028. If revenue improves while the cost base is leaner, the operating leverage could be meaningful.

That is the bull case.

### How I Think About The Scenarios

I am thinking about this in three cases.

In the bear case, glass adoption takes longer than expected. Qualification work fails to convert into meaningful production orders. Customers delay capex. LPKF remains mostly an R&D and pilot-line supplier. Solar remains weak. Welding stays uneven. Core profitability remains low. The stock gives back a large portion of the recent move.

This is possible. That is why the position is small.

In the base case, LPKF wins some production-related LIDE tools. The glass transition starts slowly. LIDE becomes meaningful but gradual. North Star improves the cost structure. Solar stabilizes or improves modestly. Welding gets cleaner as management shifts toward non-automotive markets. The company moves toward a healthier EBIT profile by 2028.

In this case, LPKF becomes a better business and the option stays alive.

In the bull case, glass-core substrates scale into AI and HPC packaging. Multiple customers move from qualification into production. LPKF remains one of the leading process suppliers. LIDE becomes a large revenue stream relative to the current company. Ablation, bonding, Vitrion, and adjacent process steps attach around the core tool. The company reaches or exceeds its double-digit EBIT margin target.

In that case, LPKF could look very different from the company investors saw in 2025. That is the reason I want exposure.

I don't have any revenue/valuation targets yet. It's too early.

### The Catalysts I Am Watching

This is the most important part for the position from here. I do not want to own this purely on vibes. I want to see the story move from qualification language into reported evidence.

The first thing I want to see is Q1 2026 earnings and order intake. LPKF's 2025 order intake was weak. If the advanced packaging story is starting to move, order intake should eventually show signs of it.

The second catalyst is any production-related LIDE order language. This is the biggest one. Qualification tools are good but production tools are better. The shift from customer evaluation into production investment is the entire thesis. I want management to give real language around production-related orders, order size, timing, and customer maturity.

The AGM is another catalyst. Management has hinted that more clarity could come as Q1 and Q2 develop. I want to hear more about how many customers are moving from qualification to early production. I want details around whether these are single tools, small clusters of tools, or larger capacity plans.

The Q2 update also matters. One quarter can be noisy, but two quarters can start to show whether this is real. By Q2, I want a better read on how much of the 2026 advanced packaging activity can convert into revenue this year versus backlog for 2027.

I am also watching the TPK / ASE TGV pilot line. This is an external milestone worth tracking. If the broader TGV ecosystem progresses, that supports the idea that glass is moving from concept toward manufacturing reality.

Beyond that, I am watching Fraunhofer, Onto, and Vitrion updates. LPKF's ecosystem position is important. Updates from Onto, Fraunhofer, Vitrion, or other advanced packaging partners can help show whether LPKF is becoming part of a wider process flow.

I also want evidence of attach beyond LIDE. This is where the thesis can get bigger. If LPKF can sell ablation, bonding, or adjacent process tools alongside LIDE, the revenue opportunity expands. I want to see proof that customers view LPKF as more than one process step.

Finally, I am watching North Star, solar, and CPO. North Star needs to lower the break-even point and improve margins. Solar could help bridge the company if perovskite investment returns. CPO-on-glass is a later-stage option if glass becomes important for optical interconnect packaging.

### The Risks

The first risk is that the stock has already moved a bit. LPKF is no longer a completely undiscovered small-cap. The glass substrate narrative spread quickly, and the stock rerated before the financials turned. That creates risk around expectations. If Q1 or Q2 lacks the order language investors want, the stock could pull back hard.

The second risk is that qualification may fail to convert. The 80% qualification claim is powerful, but qualification is still an early stage. Customers can test tools for years. They can delay production. They can choose another process. They can split orders across multiple suppliers. They can build around a supplier if the economics change. The key risk is that LPKF stays important in evaluation but fails to capture enough production revenue.

The third risk is timing. The industry can talk about glass for years before high-volume manufacturing arrives. Advanced packaging is complex. Even if LIDE works, downstream steps still have to work too. Metallization, RDL, bonding, inspection, yield learning, panel handling, and reliability all need to come together. LPKF controls one important part of the flow but it does not control the whole flow.

The fourth risk is competition. LPKF may have a strong position, but customers will want second sources. Asian competitors can price aggressively. China can favor local suppliers. Other process technologies can improve. Large laser or semiconductor equipment companies could also try to enter adjacent process steps. LPKF does not need 70% share for the stock to work, but the upside gets much smaller if share falls sharply or pricing gets pressured.

The fifth risk is the core business. This is still a low-margin industrial company today. Solar is weak. Automotive welding has been pressured. Electronics can be cyclical. Development is useful, but it cannot carry the full story alone. If the glass ramp takes longer, the core business needs to hold together.

The last risk is broader AI supply-chain sentiment. This name is now being pulled into the broader AI infrastructure trade. That helps on the way up. It also cuts the other way. If AI capex sentiment weakens, or if investors rotate out of smaller supply-chain names, LPKF can get hit even if its own long-term story remains intact.
