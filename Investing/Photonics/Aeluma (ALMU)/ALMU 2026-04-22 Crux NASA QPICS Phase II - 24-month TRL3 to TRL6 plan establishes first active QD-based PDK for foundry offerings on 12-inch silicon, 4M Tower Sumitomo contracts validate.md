### The Problem Aeluma Is Trying to Solve

You often hear this idea when you dig into Photonics as an industry. Silicon handles light extremely well once the light is on the chip. Engineers can guide it, split it, route it, and process signals with a high degree of precision. That is why silicon photonics has become such an important part of modern communications systems.

Generating and boosting the light is the tougher part.

Those jobs come from compound semiconductor materials rather than silicon. So in many optical systems, one set of materials creates the light, another platform moves it around, and then everything gets packaged together in a way that works reliably.

That packaging burden is easy to underestimate. Each extra part adds complexity. Each alignment step adds engineering work. Each separate material system increases the friction between performance, cost, and scale. As systems get faster and more demanding, that friction compounds.

Aeluma is trying to combine the strengths of compound semiconductor materials with the scale and manufacturing advantages of large silicon wafers. Their approach also points away from the photonics model built around smaller indium phosphide, or InP, wafer flows and more complex integration later on. The company is building around quantum-dot structures, InGaAs-based devices, and large silicon wafers. If the industry keeps running into cost, throughput, or supply pressure in traditional InP-heavy flows, that difference becomes a real advantage.

> **Summary Details**
>
> **Estimated Technology Readiness Level (TRL Begin - TRL End): 3 - 6**
>
> **Technical Abstract (Limit 2000 characters):**
>
> Aeluma, in collaboration with the University of Delaware, proposes to advance Quantum Dot Photonic Integrated Circuits on Silicon Photonics (QPICS) by developing scalable, high-performance quantum dot (QD) lasers and semiconductor optical amplifiers (SOAs) directly compatible with foundry-level silicon photonics platforms. Building on successful Phase I results, Phase II will leverage Aelumas proprietary 12-inch MOCVD growth technology to achieve robust epitaxy of QD materials enabling high-throughput and cost-effective manufacturing. The program will integrate these QD gain elements into silicon photonics designed by the University of Delaware and establish the first active QD-based photonic process design kits (PDKs) for foundry offerings. NASA applications include space-qualified optical communications, astronomy, docking, ranging, and sensing, where QD devices reduce size, weight, power, and cost (SWaP-C) while delivering superior performance in harsh and extreme environments. Broader dual-use markets include defense LiDAR and secure free-space optical links to satisfy NASA's urgent needs in PIC deployment, and commercial sectors such as LiDAR for autonomous vehicles, datacom transceivers, AI/high-performance computing (HPC) optical interconnects, and future quantum communication systems. Phase II funding will support QD laser and SOA growth optimization, device fabrication and iterations, testing and packaging, silicon photonic integration, and prototype demonstrations, while engaging foundries to enable scalable manufacturing. By combining domestic 12-inch MOCVD capability at Aeluma, extensive IP, and proven Phase I feasibility, this effort will deliver NASA-relevant prototypes and position the U.S. as a leader in advanced photonic integration for space, defense, and commercial markets.
>
> **Duration: 24**

A few details stand out immediately.

First, this is a Phase II program. The concept work has already been done. The focus now shifts toward scaling, integration, and building working prototypes.

Second, the scope extends beyond just creating light. The program includes quantum dot lasers, which generate light, and semiconductor optical amplifiers (SOAs), which boost optical signals once they are on-chip.

Third, everything is tied directly to foundry-level silicon photonics platforms which is the bridge between device performance and real manufacturing.

And then there is this line:

> "The program will… establish the first active QD-based photonic process design kits (PDKs) for foundry offerings."

A PDK is the framework engineers use when they design around a manufacturing process like the models, rules, and parameters that make a technology usable inside a foundry flow. A company working at the PDK level becomes part of how systems get designed, not just a supplier of individual components. This award funds the work required to make Aeluma's devices usable inside a real manufacturing ecosystem. That is a really big deal.

### The Recent Paper Shows the Platform Starting to Behave Like a Platform

After going through the award material, Stockwell found a recent paper tied to this same thread: "Quantum Dot Photodetector and Laser Monolithically Integrated on Silicon Photonics."

Read it here if you are curious:

https://pubs.acs.org/doi/10.1021/acsphotonics.5c01299

The paper demonstrates quantum dot photodetectors and quantum dot lasers monolithically integrated on a silicon photonics platform. The detail is that the devices use the same III-V layer stack, with the function changing based on bias. Forward bias - laser. Reverse bias - photodetector.

That is a significant result. The same material stack serving more than one function means more leverage from the same process flow. It moves the story away from isolated device demos toward something that looks like a reusable active-device platform.

The manufacturing angle shows up here too. The work is done on a foundry-processed silicon photonics platform compatible with 300mm fabrication, directly in line with the larger Aeluma thesis.

The paper shows pieces of the future platform already working together:

* lasers and detectors on the same silicon photonics platform
* the same material stack serving more than one function
* monolithic integration instead of a purely assembled architecture
* system behavior that looks more like a platform than a one-off component result
