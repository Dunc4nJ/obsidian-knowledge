# Crux Capital 2026-03-31 — Samsung CPO news reframed: stack story, foundries first

The market's same-day read of Samsung's CPO news ("Samsung just decided to take on the entire optical stack") misses what's actually being announced. Crux argues this is a **silicon photonics foundry platform** play, not a laser launch — and the read-through ordering matters.

## Key Takeaways

- **What Samsung is actually doing** = building a SiPh foundry platform as part of "one-stop AI solutions" (foundry + memory + advanced packaging + photonics bundled). Roadmap: platform development NOW, basic platform 2027, **mass production 2028**.
- **The 2024 disclosure already telegraphed this:** Samsung said in June 2024 it planned to introduce an all-in-one CPO-integrated AI solution in 2027. The OFC news just makes the SiPh roadmap explicit.
- **The foundry analogy.** SiPh foundry = manufacturing platform for photonic chips — provides process, design rules, PDK, libraries, wafer flow. Customer designs the PIC, foundry fabs it, packaging/fiber-attach/thermal/electronics/test follow. External laser is the layer that historically does NOT live inside the foundry.
  - **Direct comps:** [[GlobalFoundries (GFS)]] (GF Fotonix 300mm photonics+RF+CMOS platform), [[Tower Semiconductor (TSEM)]] (SiPho positioning), [[TSMC (TSM)]] (slightly different bucket but in the conversation).
- **Stack story — implications spread outward in rings:**
  1. **Foundries and photonic platforms** feel it first (Samsung enters here most directly).
  2. **Packaging and manufacturing** rises in importance (Samsung already bundling photonics with advanced packaging; GF investing in both together).
  3. **Test** becomes more consequential as SiPh/CPO moves from roadmap to production — wafer-level validation, packaging test, system-level qualification.
  4. **Materials** = second-order. Bigger SiPh/CPO ecosystem pulls more importance toward wafers, packaging substrates, optical materials.
- **Laser names = different conversation.** [[Lumentum (LITE)]]'s CPO positioning centers on ultra-high-power lasers + external light source modules. [[Coherent (COHR)]] similar. **No public evidence Samsung has a merchant external laser source product, merchant CW laser family, or any laser roadmap that would put them head-to-head with LITE/COHR in CPO-relevant lasers.** Adjacent layers, not the same business.
- **Could Samsung move into light source layer later?** Yes — if Samsung internalizes more of the light-source economics or vertically integrates the optical engine more aggressively, the overlap with merchant laser vendors becomes more significant. **But that's a later-chapter question, not the clean first read.**
- **Defensible read = ecosystem validation + some sentiment pressure.** A company Samsung's size doesn't push into a layer it thinks is trivial.
- **Timing matters.** 2027/2028 roadmap does NOT translate cleanly into 2026 revenue risk for existing optical winners. Same-day optics selloff attributing entirely to this headline is compressing too much into one story.
- **Potential near-term tailwind for laser names** — broader photonic-engine ecosystem activity tends to pull demand for high-performance light sources. The risk to LITE/COHR only sharpens IF Samsung later decides to own more of the light-source layer.
- **Long-term thesis:** if Samsung executes, the competitive fight in optical AI infrastructure may gradually shift away from "who has the best individual component" toward **"who controls the broader interconnect stack"** — foundry + memory + advanced packaging + optical engine integration + (potentially) more of the light source over time. Pressure lands on merchant foundries first; rest of supply chain comes into frame later if vertical integration keeps moving in that direction.

## Original Content

A lot of people are reading today's Samsung news as if Samsung just showed up and decided to go after the entire optical stack, and that read misses what is actually happening.

What Samsung is doing is building toward a silicon photonics foundry platform that can sit inside a much bigger AI manufacturing offering. This is not coming out of nowhere. Back in June 2024, Samsung said it planned to introduce an all-in-one CPO-integrated AI solution in 2027 as part of its broader "one-stop AI solutions" strategy, and the newer reporting around OFC makes the silicon photonics roadmap more explicit with a platform development now, a basic platform in 2027, mass production in 2028. This is a story about Samsung wanting a seat deeper in the AI hardware stack, not a story about Samsung launching a laser.

To understand this news it helps to understand what a silicon photonics foundry actually does. The foundry is a manufacturing platform for photonic chips so it provides the process, the design rules, the PDK, the libraries, and the wafer fabrication flow so customers can design and build photonic integrated circuits on top of it.

GlobalFoundries presents GF Fotonix as a 300mm platform combining photonics, RF, and CMOS on the same wafer, with design enablement and packaging support.

Tower's SiPho positioning is similar with a process platform customers use to build optical devices and engines.

The standard model is that the customer designs the chip and the foundry provides the platform and manufactures it, after which comes packaging, fiber attach, thermal management, electronics integration, test, and in some cases the connection to external lasers. That last piece is why Samsung's push is toward the foundry and platform layer rather than toward the merchant laser layer.

Samsung's strategy was laid out in 2024. They tied integrated CPO to its foundry, memory, and advanced packaging offerings under the "one-stop AI solutions" umbrella with the pitch being not just "let us fabricate a photonic wafer" but "let us help build more of the system." The SiPh move therefore carries implications beyond the photonic die itself. If Samsung gets serious here, this touches packaging, test, and manufacturing integration as well. GF's own positioning make a similar point from the other side with the value is not just the photonic integrated circuit, but the broader integration stack around it, including packaging and support for on-die integrated lasers.

The first direct read-through from all of this is to the merchant silicon photonics foundries and photonic platform players. GlobalFoundries is the clearest comp. Tower is another direct comp. TSMC belongs in the conversation too, though in a slightly different bucket.

The laser names are a different conversation enitrely. Samsung has publicly disclosed integrated CPO ambitions and a fuller AI manufacturing stack, but there is no public evidence of a merchant external laser source product, a merchant CW laser family, or any other laser roadmap that would put Samsung directly head-to-head with where Lumentum or Coherent currently play in CPO-relevant lasers. Lumentum's CPO positioning centers on ultra-high-power lasers and external light source modules feeding silicon photonics optical engines. Samsung's current push is about platform, foundry, and system-level integration. Those are adjacent layers, not the same business.

Could Samsung move closer to that layer later? Yes if it decides to internalize more of the light-source economics or vertically integrate the optical engine more aggressively, the overlap with merchant laser vendors becomes more significant. But that is a later-chapter question, not the clean first read on today's news.

This is a stack story and the implications spread outward in rings.

Foundries and photonic platforms feel it first, since that is where Samsung is entering most directly.

Packaging and manufacturing matter more as a consequence where Samsung is already bundling photonics with advanced packaging, and GF has been investing in packaging and photonics capacity together, so if more photonic engines move toward real deployment, that integration layer becomes more important across the board.

Test becomes more consequential as well, because the more silicon photonics and CPO move from roadmap to production, the more the ecosystem needs wafer-level validation, packaging test, and system-level qualification.

Materials are a more second-order piece, but a bigger SiPh and CPO ecosystem does pull more importance toward wafers, packaging substrates, optical materials, and integration inputs.

So I think the most defensible read right now is ecosystem validation with some sentiment pressure layered on top. Samsung wanting in confirms that silicon photonics has strategic significance in AI infrastructure as a company this large does not push into a layer like this because it thinks the opportunity is trivial.

But the roadmap under discussion is 2027 and 2028, which does not translate cleanly into 2026 revenue risk for the existing optical winners.

When investors attribute a same-day optics selloff entirely to this headline, they are compressing too much into one story. The headline is real and the long-term implications are real, but the near-term earnings impact across the optical complex is being overstated.

For the laser names, a broader photonic-engine ecosystem can actually be a near-term tailwind, since more optical engine activity tends to pull demand for high-performance light sources. The risk to LITE and COHR only sharpens if Samsung later decides it wants to own more of the light-source layer, which is a meaningfully different statement from "Samsung news is bearish for optics today."

Zooming out, if Samsung executes, the competitive fight in optical AI infrastructure may gradually shift away from who has the best individual component and toward who controls the broader interconnect stack with foundry, memory, advanced packaging, optical engine integration, and potentially more of the light-source layer over time.

But pressure lands on merchant foundries first. The rest of the supply chain comes into frame later if vertical integration keeps moving in that direction. Samsung is not suddenly competing with the entire optical stack. What Samsung is signaling is a future where more of the silicon photonics value chain could migrate toward a smaller number of much larger full-stack players.
