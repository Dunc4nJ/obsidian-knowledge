### Thesis

At the recent SXSW conference, Matthew Prince (CEO Cloudflare) made a very specific point. He said Cloudflare expects bot traffic to exceed human traffic online by 2027, and he explained why AI agents can visit vastly more websites than a human completing the same task. In the shopping example he used, a person might check a handful of sites, while an agent can fan out across thousands. One unit of user intent is starting to generate a much larger volume of machine-created traffic across the internet.

The read-through for a couple of my photonics positioned companies is pretty direct. If AI agents turn one request into a much larger chain of retrieval, routing, verification, and response traffic, pressure moves outward into the network. More traffic has to move between data centers, across campuses, across metros, across regions, and eventually across the broader internet edge. That favors the layer that carries and manages that traffic. In this setup, Ciena looks like the cleanest first-order beneficiary. Nokia looks like the broader second-order beneficiary.

---

### **What Prince actually said**

The old internet was dominated by humans browsing, clicking, and consuming information directly. The next version looks more machine-active. A user gives an AI system a task, and the system fans out across the web, pulls in data, checks multiple sources, calls tools, and sends results back. That multiplies requests even when the human behavior at the front end still looks simple. So this is more than AI traffic rising. This is about AI traffic scaling differently than human traffic. As Prince put it, that is "real traffic" and "real load."

For the optical and IP infrastructure angle, the model has to be trained, the inference has to run, the agent has to fetch information, the results have to move, and those workloads increasingly live across many physical locations. Once that happens, traffic between sites becomes part of the AI stack itself. And if that traffic keeps compounding, then, in Prince's words, "the infrastructure that supports that traffic is going to have to continue to be invested in. It's going to have to continue to grow."

### Alpha

The next layer here is where the spend shows up and what actually breaks first.

One thing I find especially interesting is that part of this buildout may hit service-provider infrastructure earlier than people expect. Ciena is describing a world where hyperscaler and neoscaler demand does not only translate into direct cloud transport spend. It also flows through managed optical fiber networks, where cloud providers and service providers are increasingly building connectivity together. This means some AI demand may show up in carrier transport budgets without looking like a traditional carrier cycle at all. It is hyperscaler AI demand pulling fiber and optical investment into the network through a different channel.

The second layer is that this is becoming less of a pure bandwidth story and more of a physical densification story. Ciena's Hyper-Rail framing is basically saying the real problem is fitting AI-scale transport into actual huts, racks, power envelopes, and fiber counts. Their own example shows a 20-petabit AI network going from 22 huts under today's architecture to 1 hut under a new multi-rail design, with 32x density improvement. Nokia is pointing to the same shift from a different angle. Its OFC deck explicitly says optical innovation is no longer only about maximizing capacity per fiber, but also about power, cost, space, and multi-fiber optimization, and its multi-fiber in-line amplifier slide shows 8x density improvement as part of that push. That is important because it suggests the winners here may not just be the companies with faster optics, but the ones solving the deployment problem at system level.

The third layer is manufacturing depth. Both companies are effectively saying the ecosystem still has to be built out to meet what is coming. Ciena is spending heavily to secure long-term supply and expand manufacturing capacity with partners because demand is already pressing against industry readiness. Nokia is making a similar point from the other side, saying optical still does not run at semiconductor-style volume, that the ecosystem needs more investment, and that its indium phosphide manufacturing footprint matters as demand ramps. So part of the edge here may simply be who has enough capacity, ecosystem control, and manufacturing readiness to actually deliver into the opportunity. In a market like this, that can become just as important as product leadership.
