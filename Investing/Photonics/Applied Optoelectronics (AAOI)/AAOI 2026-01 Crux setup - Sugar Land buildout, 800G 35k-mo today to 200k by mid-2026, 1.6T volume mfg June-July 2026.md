## Original Content

$AAOI has been on my radar for a long time. If you follow me, you know I'm drawn to businesses with a "boring" core and upside optionality that can reprice quickly when execution starts showing up in the numbers. For Applied Optoelectronics, that core has been CATV and broadband infrastructure, and the upside I'm underwriting is the AI data center optics stack.

AI is rebuilding data center connectivity, and AAOI sits in the part of the stack where progress shows up as shipments, yields, delivery cadence, and repeat orders. This is an execution-led position for me, and I size it that way.

---

### Two revenue engines

AAOI has two meaningful revenue engines.

The first is data center optics: transceivers and components that move data over fiber at 100G, 400G, 800G, and into 1.6T. The second is CATV and broadband infrastructure, which has produced strong revenue and cash flow in this cycle. I like the mix because it keeps the manufacturing complex loaded while the company invests into higher-speed data center capacity.

That matters because the AI optics ramp rewards suppliers who can deliver at volume with stable quality, then do it again next quarter.

---

### What I actually like about the model

The core appeal here is vertical integration paired with automation.

AAOI produces key laser components internally and has built manufacturing processes designed for scale. At 800G and 1.6T, execution separates vendors, and the scoreboard looks very specific:

* shipping in volume
* staying inside power targets
* maintaining quality
* repeating it quarter after quarter

That's what earns durable placement inside hyperscaler supply chains.

---

### Sugar Land is the center of the thesis

The footprint is central to my thesis. AAOI's manufacturing anchor is Sugar Land, Texas, and management frames the buildout there as a push toward meaningful domestic capacity for advanced transceivers.

They also run operations in Taipei and Ningbo, and their posture is to pull more advanced production toward North America as customers emphasize supply chain lineage, domestic capacity, and lower China-origin content.

The signals I watch are operational:

* capacity additions
* test readiness
* yield control
* consistent output as speeds step higher

---

### Where we are in the speed cycle

On the product cycle, the speed transitions are sequential.

400G remains a large throughput layer across deployed networks while 800G scales. 800G is the qualification and ramp phase where reliability, thermal performance, and testing discipline show up at volume. 1.6T is the next planning horizon, and it pulls harder on power efficiency and integration choices.

My focus stays on whether AAOI turns 800G volume into repeatability, because that's what expands credibility as customers design and qualify 1.6T deployments.

One thing I like in the framing: management has been consistent that 800G and 1.6T can share production lines, with differences concentrated in final test equipment. That supports the idea that today's capex can remain productive across multiple generations, instead of being a single-cycle build.

---

### Roadmap signals I'm watching

I also track the roadmap choices. AAOI has highlighted silicon photonics and referenced approaches like TFLN (thin-film lithium niobate) and silicon nitride as bandwidth and power demands rise.

I read that as an effort to align with where modulation and photonic integration are heading as the industry pushes beyond 800G and into 1.6T-era requirements. It supports the view that AAOI is preparing for the next transition while participating in the current one.

---

### Financial setup

Financially, I focus on what the mix shift implies.

Higher-speed data center optics can lift revenue per unit and improve factory absorption when output becomes steady. CATV provides operating support during the buildout, while the data center ramp can drive mix and margin improvement when execution stays consistent.

### Some financial underwriting

I want a numerical frame that ties directly to the execution checklist. I treat these as guideposts for capacity, timing, and cadence, and I update the view as the company reports.

#### Baseline performance and mix

They reported Q3 2025 revenue of $118.6M, up 82% year over year and up 15% sequentially. They described mix at roughly 60% CATV, 37% data center, and 3% other, and quantified CATV at $70.6M with data center at $43.9M.

Non-GAAP gross margin was 31%, with a long-term goal around 40% as mix and scale evolve. They also quantified concentration: one CATV customer at 66% of total revenue and one data center customer at 24%.

They said approximately $6.6M of 400G transceiver shipments to a hyperscale customer missed Q3 revenue recognition due to shipping and receiving timing, and the company booked it into Q4. I treat that as a clean datapoint for shipment flow and revenue capture.

They guided Q4 2025 revenue at $125M to $140M and guided Q4 CATV at $50M to $55M. That implies data center plus other around $70M to $90M for Q4, depending on where the quarter lands.

Inside that, management framed initial 800G single-mode contribution for Q4 at about $4M to $8M, with the larger sequential growth driven by 400G.

They laid out an advanced transceiver capacity ramp for 800G and 1.6T, describing:

* a nearer-term milestone of roughly 35,000 advanced transceivers per month
* a mid-2026 expectation of over 200,000 pieces per month
* "majority produced in Texas" as the stated goal

I view that as the scale ambition for Sugar Land, and I watch for equipment, staffing, test capacity, and yield signals that track the same direction.

They also put 400G single-mode into unit terms. The CEO said Q4 400G single-mode output capability was close to 60,000 per month, and framed a target by Q2 of next year of about 110,000 to 120,000 per month, tied to capacity additions in Taiwan and the U.S.

#### 1.6T timeline

Management described active work with around four customers on 1.6T single-mode, framed sample delivery end of the year or early next year, and discussed volume manufacturing around June to July next year for 1.6T, with multiple 1.6T products in development.

#### Laser supply and scaling plan

Management emphasized internal laser capacity and described scaling, including moving from 3-inch to 4-inch wafers next year, a target of at least 2 million lasers per month by December next year, and a 6-inch wafer effort framed as a longer project timeline.

I treat internal lasers as a strategic advantage when laser availability and pricing influence module availability.

#### Funding posture

They reported ending Q3 with $150.7M in total cash, cash equivalents, short-term investments, and restricted cash, and total debt excluding convertibles of $62M. Inventory was $170.2M, tied to raw material purchases for the coming months.

They also described raising about $147M net via an ATM during the quarter, and capex of $49.9M in Q3, with year-to-date capex at $124.9M. Their prior annual capex framing was $120M to $150M, and they described tracking at or above that range as equipment delivery timing evolves.

I don't translate unit targets into a single-point revenue forecast because module ASPs vary by form factor, reach, optics content, and customer mix.

I do translate them into a directionally investable setup: if AAOI moves from early advanced-module capacity into the mid-2026 scale they described, and yields plus test discipline hold, the model can shift meaningfully because higher-speed mix lifts revenue per unit and drives factory absorption.
