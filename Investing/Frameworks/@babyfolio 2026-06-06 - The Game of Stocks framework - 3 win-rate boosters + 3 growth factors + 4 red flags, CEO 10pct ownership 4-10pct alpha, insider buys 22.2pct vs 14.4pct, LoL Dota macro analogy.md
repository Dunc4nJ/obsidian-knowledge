## Key Takeaways

- **CEO ownership >10% = 4-10% annual alpha** vs firms with zero CEO ownership (the load-bearing skin-in-the-game statistic). Combined with founder-led status, **"the most powerful structural advantage in the market"** — visionary capital allocator (R&D-heavy, focused acquisitions, willing to sacrifice short-term earnings for long-term dominance) AND the maximum financial-risk-bearer in the same person.
- **Insider buys 22.2% vs insider sells 14.4% over following 12 months** (the load-bearing insider-trading anomaly statistic). Insiders sell for dozens of reasons (taxes, diversification, buying a house); insiders **buy for only one reason** — they believe the stock is undervalued and is about to go up. The asymmetry is structural, not statistical noise.
- **TAM expansion is the "favorable terrain"** — author names three explicit theme buckets where the entire pie is growing: **AI-critical memory** (cross-links to the Memory sector: [[Kioxia (285A.T)]], [[SK Hynix (000660.KS)]], [[Micron (MU)]]), **liquid cooling technologies** ([[Vertiv (VRT)]]), and **humanoid robotics** (Robotics sector hubs [[Harmonic Drive Systems (6324)]] / [[Nabtesco (6268.T)]] / [[Nidec (6594.T)]] / [[Leaderdrive (688017.SH)]]). > # I have been a gamer my entire life.
> For years, I viewed the stock market as this unpredictable beast.
> But once I started treating it like a strategic game of probability and data, winning actually started to feel structural rather than lucky.
>
> If you have ever played competitive strategy games like League of Legends or Dota 2, you know that every single decision matters.
> Your ultimate goal is to raise your win rate as much as possible by optimizing your strategy, making the right macro-level decisions, and choosing the right factors before the match even begins.
>
> How do we apply this to the stock market? Some people call the market a casino. But gambling relies on blind luck.
>
> In the stock market, you have data on your side. If you look at the right data points, you can increase your win rate by a massive margin.
> Just like poor strategy can ruin your chances of success, certain financial metrics act as permanent headwinds to your portfolio, while others act as structural tailwinds.
>
> Relying on data points like capital allocation, management incentives, and business fundamentals is one of the few proven ways to tilt the odds in your favor.
> When screening for high-conviction investments, layering these factors on top of your fundamental thesis is essentially free additional "win rate".
>
> Here is the breakdown of the metrics, structural advantages, and red flags you need to track to build an unbeatable portfolio.
>
> These are the data points that historically drive heavy outperformance. Consider these your foundational win rate boosters.
>
> **- The Founder Led Companies:**
> Firms run by their founders allocate capital differently than professional corporate CEOs.
> Founders invest more heavily in research and development, make highly focused acquisitions, and are totally willing to sacrifice short-term earnings to secure long-term market dominance.
>
> **- Skin in the Game (Alignment):** While the founder effect is powerful, raw ownership percentage is an independent and highly lucrative driver of returns.
> Investing in firms where the CEO owns more than 10% of the outstanding shares delivers an annual alpha of 4% to 10% compared to firms with zero CEO ownership ownership perfectly aligns the executive team's financial incentives with outside shareholders.
> They literally only win when you win.
>
> When you combine these two metrics (Founder led + Skin in the game) you find the most powerful structural advantage in the market.
> A company led by its original founder who also holds a massive equity stake creates the ultimate alignment.
> This combination guarantees that the visionary driving the aggressive, long-term capital allocation is also the one carrying the most financial risk. They are not just managing the company; they own it. This ensures that every dollar spent is designed to maximize your shared returns, building an almost unbeatable structural edge.
>
> **- Insider Trading Anomalies:** Insiders sell shares for dozens of reasons like tax obligations, diversification, or buying a house.
> But they only buy shares for one reason: they believe the stock is undervalued and is about to go up.
> Firms with extensive insider purchases over a six-month period returned an average of 22.2% over the following 12 months, compared to only 14.4% for firms with extensive insider sales
>
> ## The Growth Factors
>
> A statistical edge is great, but if you want to see massive returns, your company needs to scale. These fundamental data points act as your primary momentum drivers.
>
> **- Sector Growth (TAM Expansion):** In any strategic game, fighting on favorable terrain gives you an automatic advantage. In the stock market, this is your Total Addressable Market (TAM) expansion.
> If you invest in a stagnant industry, your company has to ruthlessly steal market share from competitors just to grow. But if you invest in a sector with explosive TAM expansion, such as AI-critical memory, liquid cooling technologies, or the humanoid robotics market, the entire pie is getting bigger.
> A rising tide lifts all boats, providing your stock a massive tailwind even if management makes minor missteps.
>
> **- Projected Revenue Growth:** Top-line revenue growth is the raw engine of your company.
> Cost-cutting can only boost earnings for so long before you hit a wall. You want companies that are consistently projecting and delivering double-digit, accelerating revenue growth.
> Think of the specialized AI infrastructure space or neo-cloud providers rapidly scaling their megawatt capacity.
> this proves they have a product the market desperately needs and the ability to capture new enterprise customers quickly.
>
> **- Margin Expansion (Operating Leverage):** This is one of the most powerful mechanics in finance.
> Margin expansion happens when a company's revenue grows faster than its costs.
> Imagine a specialized hardware company investing heavily to build out a new manufacturing facility.
> The initial capital expenditure is massive, but once production scales, the cost per unit plummets. As sales increase, their Gross Margins and Operating Margins widen.
> This creates "operating leverage," meaning a 15% increase in revenue might translate to a 40% increase in net income.
> It is how you secure the ultimate fundamental re-rate.
>
> ## The Red Flags: Avoiding Value Traps
>
> Every system has traps and structural flaws that drain your capital.
> The stock market is no different.
> If you see these red flags on a company's balance sheet, avoid the trade entirely.
>
> **- Share Dilution:**
> When a company constantly issues new shares to raise cash, they are diluting the value of the equity you already own. However, it is important to note that dilution is not universally bad.
> It acts as a structural trap primarily when the company generates a low Return on Investment (ROI) on that newly raised cash or deploys it into very risky, speculative assets.
>
> If management issues equity just to fund low-return projects or keep the lights on, it permanently suppresses your potential returns.
> Run away from chronic diluters who cannot prove a high, risk-adjusted return on the capital they raise.
>
> **- High Debt:**
> Leverage can be useful, but too much debt acts like a permanent anchor on cash flow.
> Track the "Interest Coverage Ratio," which measures how easily a company can pay interest on its outstanding debt.
> If a company is using all of its operating income just to service debt, they have no capital left to deploy for R&D, marketing, or strategic acquisitions.
> When interest rates rise, this debt burden accelerates, often leading to restructuring or bankruptcy.
>
> **- Declining Margins:**
> If revenue is going up but profit margins are shrinking, the company is bleeding out. This usually means they are slashing prices just to survive, or their supply chain costs are completely out of control. It is a glaring signal that they have no economic "moat" or pricing power. They are deploying more capital just to stay in the exact same place.
>
> **- Insider Mass Exodus:**
> While an insider selling a few shares for taxes is normal, a coordinated mass exodus is a massive red flag.
> If the CEO, CFO, and lead directors are all liquidating huge chunks of their stock at the exact same time, they know the core thesis is fundamentally broken.
> They see the bad news coming before the public does.
> Never provide exit liquidity for executives running for the door.
>
> Treating the stock market like a game does not mean taking it lightly.
> It means respecting the math, the data, and the probability.
> By building your strategy around these proven data points and avoiding the obvious value traps, you stop relying on luck and start playing with a structural edge.
> Align your factors, verify the data, and watch your win rate climb.
>
