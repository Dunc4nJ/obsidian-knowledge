> Article: Part 2. The HELW Counter-Drone Franchise
>
> About this series.
>
> EOS is a small-cap Australian defence and space company with several things going on at once. There is a mature, cash-generating Remote Weapon System business, branded internally as Firepower and Force Protection. There is a high-energy laser weapons (HELW) capability that is being applied across two distinct end-markets, namely terrestrial counter-drone defence and the directed-energy anti-satellite mission. And there is a CEO, Andreas Schwer, whose personal background and governance philosophy are central to whether any of this becomes a coherent investment thesis. Trying to say anything coherent about the equity in a single document would be either too long or too thin. So this essay is built in four parts.
>
> Part 1 introduced the company and walked through the Remote Weapon System franchise in detail. Part 2, the chapter you are reading, picks up the high-energy laser weapon counter-drone business. It opens with a short history of HELW as a category, traces the path EOS itself took from satellite tracking into directed energy, and then examines Apollo, the Korean Goldrone contract and the Grizzly Research short report, the wider competitive set across the United States, Europe and Israel, and the MARSS acquisitions that converted EOS, on management's framing, from a component supplier into a counter-drone systems prime. Part 3 covers the HELW anti-satellite business and the directed-energy space-situational-awareness mission that the same laser technology supports. Part 4 is the Andreas Schwer chapter, which examines the leadership question, the ongoing governance overhang, and ties the previous three operational chapters back to a unified investment view of the equity. Each part is designed to stand alone, but they are more useful read in order.
>
> # Executive Summary
>
> Three things to take away. The first is that, in roughly twelve months, EOS has gone from a directed-energy R&D house with a credible technology stack to the only company in the world holding a signed export contract for a deployable 100 kW-class laser weapon. The Royal Netherlands Army order of August 2025 is the anchor: €71.4m, one mobile Apollo system, deliveries through 2028, manufacturing at the new Singapore facility that opened in February 2026. Around it sit a binding-conditional US$80m follow-on with Korea's Goldrone, strategic partnerships with KNDS France, ROKETSAN and Diehl Defence, and active negotiations with what Schwer describes as ten further European governments. What makes all of this commercially possible is that Apollo is ITAR-free. Every non-Israeli, non-American 100 kW laser project on the planet is constrained by U.S. or Israeli export-control politics. Apollo is not.
>
> The second observation is that the Grizzly Research short report of 6 February 2026 is an attack on counterparty quality rather than on EOS's technology, balance sheet, or governance of the contract itself. Grizzly's forensics on Goldrone are largely accurate. EOS did not contest them on substance. What Grizzly does not establish, and cannot establish given how the contract is structured, is that EOS carries material economic downside if Goldrone fails to perform. The contract is conditional on a US$18m cash deposit, a bank Letter of Credit for the residual US$62m, and customer acceptance of the Singapore facility. None of these obliges EOS to commit production resources beforehand. The worst realistic outcome is that the contract simply does not convert, which removes a future revenue line but does not impair the A$518m unconditional order book at 31 March 2026, the Netherlands deliverable, or the A$9.5m of positive operating cash flow EOS posted in Q1 2026. Bell Potter, Canaccord Genuity and Ord Minnett all maintain Buy ratings as of late April 2026 with price targets between A$10.40 and A$12.95, which is the price action of a market treating Goldrone as an option whose loss is asymmetric in EOS's favour.
>
> The third observation is that the MARSS acquisitions of late 2025 and early 2026 have changed the shape of the company. The €5.5m Interceptor deal in November 2025 and the US$36m up-front plus up-to-€100m earnout deal for the rest of the group in January 2026 brought into the EOS stack a high-speed AI-guided counter-drone interceptor (the Interceptor-MR), a portable shoulder-launched variant (the Interceptor-SR), and the NiDAR sensor-fusion and command-and-control platform deployed at more than sixty sites globally. The result is a counter-drone architecture in which a single integrated provider can offer detection through NiDAR, soft-kill through electronic warfare partners, hard-kill through Slinger at ammunition economics of US$155 to US$1,550 per engagement, hard-kill through Apollo at sub-dollar marginal cost, and reusable kinetic interception through the Interceptor-MR at US$30,000 to US$40,000 per shot against missile alternatives that cost five times more. The credible peer group above EOS at the systems-prime level is the integrated U.S. and European counter-drone primes, a tougher set than the gun-RWS competitor list of Part 1, and management's bet is that the ITAR-free, software-plus-hardware combination is good enough to compete in it.

> # 1.  A History of High-Energy Laser Weapons
> 
> HELW has been a category for forty years. For most of those forty years it was a chronicle of near-misses. What changed in the late 2010s and early 2020s is what makes 100 kW-class laser weapons something armies are now actually deploying, and the short version of that change runs through three eras. The first was chemical, expensive, and impractical. The second was solid-state and promising but underpowered. The third is fibre-based, deployable, and entering operational service in 2025 and 2026. EOS sits at the leading edge of the third era as a small, technically credible player whose architectural choices were made specifically to dodge the constraints that held back its larger U.S. and European peers.
> 
> ## 1.1  The Chemical-Laser Era. 1980 to 2010
> 
> The lineage of the modern laser weapon runs back through the U.S. Strategic Defense Initiative announced by
> 
> President Reagan in March 1983. SDI funded a generation of megawatt-class chemical devices, of which the Mid-Infrared Advanced Chemical Laser at White Sands Missile Range was the most consequential. MIRACL first lased in 1980, ran on deuterium-fluoride combustion chemistry capable of more than a megawatt of continuous-wave output for up to seventy seconds, and successfully destroyed a tethered Titan booster casing in 1985. That was the first widely reported HELW kill. DARPA's own narrative now treats MIRACL as the generative project that produced both the Tactical High Energy Laser, a U.S.-Israeli effort that in 2000 and 2001 intercepted twenty-eight supersonic Katyusha rockets and five artillery shells over White Sands, and the U.S. Air Force Airborne Laser, a Chemical Oxygen-Iodine Laser installed in a modified Boeing 747-400F to attempt boost-phase intercept of intercontinental ballistic missiles.
> 
> Each of these programmes failed the same operational test. Chemical lasers required logistically prohibitive fuel chains. The fuels were hypergolic, the exhausts were toxic, and the no-go zones around an emplaced THEL trailer or an airborne ABL ran to the kilometre. They generated waste heat on a scale impractical for tactical platforms. They saturated cost-effectiveness limits long before they reached production. THEL's hardware was effectively a trailer park and the system was never fielded. ABL never demonstrated operationally relevant range against a hardened target and was retired in 2014. The U.S. Navy's quiet pivot in the 1990s, first to free-electron-laser research and then in the 2000s to fibre lasers, reflected the same practical conclusion that everyone working on chemical weapon lasers eventually reached. The chemistry was a dead end as a weapon-system architecture. Whatever came next would have to be electric.
> 
> ## 1.2  The Solid-State and Fibre Transition. 2010 to 2020
> 
> The architectural breakthrough that made HELW deployment-viable was the maturation of high-power fibre lasers in the late 2000s and early 2010s. Industrial fibre lasers, initially commercialised for cutting and welding, produced individual modules in the 2 to 20 kW range with diffraction-limited beam quality. The remaining engineering challenge was combination. How do you add multiple modules into one weapon-grade beam at more than 50 kW while preserving beam quality through atmospheric turbulence and platform vibration? Two broad solutions emerged. Spectral beam combining uses a diffraction grating to merge modules operating at slightly different wavelengths into a co-propagating output. It is mechanically simpler and is the architecture chosen by EOS for Apollo, by Rheinmetall and MBDA Deutschland for the Sachsen demonstrator, and by Lockheed Martin for HELIOS. Coherent beam combining phase-locks multiple modules so that their electromagnetic waves align constructively. It is more theoretically efficient but requires real-time adaptive-optics phase correction, and is the route taken by nLight under the U.S. HELSI programme and reportedly by the UK DragonFire consortium.
> 
> Either way, the displacement of chemical fuels by ship-grid or vehicle-grid electricity, plus the maturation of gallium-nitride power electronics, reduced the marginal cost per shot from cannon-round economics to single-digit dollars in electricity. The official cost-per-shot figures now circulating in industry marketing materials run from £10 for the UK DragonFire, through approximately US$1 for Apollo, to roughly US$3.50 for Iron Beam. These figures refer only to electricity and conceal capital costs of tens of millions of dollars per emplacement plus annual sustainment. But the order-of-magnitude shift in marginal economics is what made the entire category commercially interesting. Against an Iranian Shahed drone that costs in the low tens of thousands of dollars to manufacture, a Patriot PAC-3 costs roughly US$4m. A laser engagement at single-digit dollars is the ammunition-economics revolution that the Pentagon, the British and German MoDs and the Israeli Defense Ministry have all separately concluded justifies the capital cost.
> 
> ## 1.3  The Current Generation. Demonstrators and Operational Systems
> 
> Boeing's High Energy Laser Mobile Demonstrator was a 10 kW solid-state laser on a Heavy Expanded Mobility Tactical Truck, demonstrated at White Sands in 2013. It engaged more than ninety 60 mm mortar rounds and several UAVs, scaled successively to 50 kW, and had a road-map to 100 kW. Boeing did not graduate the system to an Army programme of record. HEL-MD remains historically important because it was the first credible demonstration that a mobile solid-state HEL could perform a counter-rocket-artillery-mortar mission. The U.S. Navy's LaWS, a 30 kW spectral-beam-combined laser, deployed to the Persian Gulf in August 2014 on USS Ponce and was declared an operational asset by U.S. Central Command in December 2014. It shot down a ScanEagle UAV, engaged small-boat targets in live fleet conditions, was operated by a sailor with what was widely described as an Xbox-style controller, and remained on Ponce until the ship's 2017 decommissioning. LaWS was a demonstrator rather than a fielded weapon, but it removed any remaining doubt that lasers could survive the maritime environment.
> 
> The U.S. Army Directed Energy Manoeuvre Short-Range Air Defence programme put a 50 kW class laser on a Stryker, built around a coherent-beam-combined nLight source and a KORD beam director. Initial deployments to soldier hands-on assessments in U.S. Central Command from 2021 onward were promising. The Congressional Research Service's March 2026 update found that lab performance had not transferred to tactical conditions and the programme had been redirected toward an Enduring HEL successor. The 300 kW Indirect Fires Protection Capability High-Energy Laser programme has likewise been narrowed from four prototypes to one, with no fielding currently planned, and folded into the broader Joint Laser Warfighting System under the Pentagon's Golden Dome architecture. The U.S. Navy's Optical Dazzling Interdictor Navy and High Energy
> 
> Laser with Integrated Optical-dazzler and Surveillance, the latter built by Lockheed Martin and delivered to the Navy in 2022 for installation on USS Preble, are the more credible operational endpoints. HELIOS is a 60-plus kW SBC laser scalable to 120 kW, integrated with the Aegis combat system. As of the 2025 Sea-Air-Space exposition, Vice CNO Adm. James Kilby stated publicly that the Navy was not ready to commit to HELIOS production until in-action reliability was confirmed. Lockheed announced first light on its 50 kW DEIMOS system in 2026.
> 
> The UK's DragonFire programme, run by an MBDA UK, Leonardo UK, QinetiQ and Dstl consortium, produced a 50 kW-class fibre-combined laser. Successive trials at the Hebrides Range in 2024 and 2025 demonstrated above-the-horizon engagement of drones at speeds up to 650 km per hour. In November 2025 the UK Ministry of Defence signed a £316m production contract with MBDA for two units. Defence Minister Vernon Coaker confirmed in March 2026 that the first system would be installed on a Type 45 destroyer in 2027, five years ahead of the original 2032 timetable, and explicitly ahead of any continental European NATO peer. The official cost per shot is £10. The German naval laser demonstrator, a 20 kW SBC system produced by the MBDA Deutschland and Rheinmetall Waffe Munition consortium, was mounted in a 20-foot ISO container on the F124 frigate Sachsen. More than one hundred live firings across six campaigns engaged drones in operationally representative conditions. The demonstrator was transferred to the Bundeswehr's WTD 91 facility at Meppen in October 2025 for land-based qualification. An operational German Navy laser is now targeted for 2029, with scaling to a 100 kW class.
> 
> The single most important reference point in this competitive set is Israel's Iron Beam. Rafael developed the system in cooperation with the Israeli Ministry of Defense's DDR&D unit over a thirteen-plus-year cycle. Iron Beam is a 100 kW fibre laser declared operational by the Israeli MoD in mid-September 2025. The first IDF battery, designated Or Eitan, was formally handed over on 28 December 2025. The smaller Lite Beam at 10 kW and the Iron Beam-M mobile variant at 50 kW are at earlier stages. Of greater significance for the operational record, in June 2025 the Defence Ministry disclosed that lower-power laser systems had shot down approximately forty Hezbollah drones in October 2024, and during the forty-day Iran conflict that began in February 2026 (Israeli figures, 550 ballistic and cruise missiles plus 2,200 drones launched at Gulf targets),
> 
> Israel reportedly forward-deployed Iron Dome and a version of Iron Beam to the United Arab Emirates. The Jerusalem Post acknowledged in March 2026 that Iron Beam itself saw only limited use in the Iran war due to a small number of fielded batteries. Rafael CEO Yoav Tourgeman has publicly described jaw-dropping intercepts but Rafael has not published a numerical wartime kill record
> 
> Russian and Ukrainian systems sit on the periphery of the verifiable record. The 2018 Peresvet laser is generally assessed by Western analysts as a satellite-dazzler associated with mobile ICBM regiments, not a hard-kill weapon. Russian Deputy Prime Minister Yury Borisov claimed in May 2022 that the more powerful Zadira was burning Ukrainian drones. No independent confirmation has emerged. Pro-Russian channels have circulated combat footage in 2025, but the systems remain unverified as operational HEL weapons in any Western OSINT consensus. On the Ukrainian side, Colonel Vadym Sukharevskyi disclosed in March 2025 that a domestically developed laser called Tryzub had engaged low-flying targets including Shahed-class drones. Specifications and confirmed kills are classified.
> 
> Sum it up like this. As of 2026, only Israel's Iron Beam has crossed the line from demonstrator to operationally fielded HELW, and even there the combat record is partially classified and partially limited by the small number of batteries built. DragonFire and the German naval demonstrator are credible but unfielded. HELIOS, ODIN and DE M-SHORAD are installed on platforms but not yet engaging targets in routine service. Boeing HEL-MD and U.S. Navy LaWS are historic milestones. The Russian and Ukrainian systems are largely unverified. Apollo, the EOS product introduced in §3 below, is the only ITAR-free 100 kW system in the world holding signed export contracts.

> ## 2.1  From Mount Stromlo to Apollo
> 
> EOS was founded in 1983 through the privatisation of part of Australia's satellite tracking programme. From 1995 the company operated laser ranging systems at Mount Stromlo Observatory in the Australian Capital Territory, and later at Learmonth in Western Australia, providing satellite-laser-ranging data to Geoscience Australia from 2004 as part of the International Laser Ranging Service network. In 2002 the company contracted with the U.S. Air Force on the RazorView programme to demonstrate high-precision tracking of small space debris with lasers. From 2014 it ran the A$62m Space Environment Research Centre cooperative research programme with the ANU, RMIT, NICT in Japan, and Lockheed Martin Space Systems, demonstrating laser guide-star adaptive optics and photon-pressure debris mitigation. That last phrase is worth pausing on. Photon-pressure debris mitigation means the controlled use of a high-power laser to nudge a small object in low Earth orbit. The engineering required to do that, namely high-power lasers, beam directors, adaptive optics, and sub-arcsecond tracking through atmospheric turbulence, is the same engineering required for an HELW. EOS has effectively been a directed-energy company for forty years. The defence application is the recent commercial expression of a much older technical heritage.
> 
> The Apollo product line itself was built in the early 2020s on this foundation. EOS publicly demonstrated a 34 kW configuration of the system at the Klondyke Range Complex, around 800 km north-west of Sydney, over three weeks in August 2023. Visiting delegations included representatives of the Australian Defence Force and foreign defence attachés from Europe, the Middle East, North America and South-East Asia. The system, housed in a shipping container, destroyed small quadcopter drones at ranges out to 1,000 metres and engaged targets out to 1,500 metres. EOS describes the Netherlands order as a scale-up of existing technology rather than a prototype. The 100 kW class system was unveiled under the Apollo brand at DSEI UK on 8 September 2025, three weeks after the Royal Netherlands Army contract. Schwer's framing in subsequent interviews is that EOS is the only company worldwide that can offer 100 kW systems and manufacture them locally in the customer's country, because it owns all the necessary intellectual property rights. The architecture combines multiple ytterbium-doped fibre modules through a diffraction grating into a single 100 kW output. The fibre modules are sourced outside the United States, which is the precondition for the ITAR-free claim that is the entire commercial premise of the system.

> ## 2.3  The Hard Engineering
> 
> Even with fibre architectures, deploying a 100 kW HELW runs into a stack of engineering constraints that are not going away. Atmospheric attenuation and turbulence in humid, dusty or maritime conditions is why Iron Beam's seven-kilometre clear-weather range collapses in fog. Dwell time per kill matters next: Apollo's sub-1.5 second figure for a Group 1 UAV implies twenty to thirty drones a minute, which depends entirely on slew rate and target hand-off and degrades fast against hardened targets. Thermal management is brutal. A 100 kW laser at roughly 50 percent wall-plug efficiency generates around 200 kW of waste heat to dissipate from a truck-sized footprint. Beam control and adaptive optics is the single most demanding sub-system and the place where most U.S. lab-to-field disappointments have surfaced. Target acquisition and tracking, the radar plus EO/IR plus C2 stack, is where the bottleneck has shifted, more than on the laser itself. Power generation constrains shot economics on platforms without ship-grid or grid-tie support, and the headline cost-per-shot figures of £10, US$1 and US$3.50 for DragonFire, Apollo and Iron Beam refer only to electricity. They hide capital costs of tens of millions per emplacement and meaningful annual sustainment. None of this is fatal. All of it defines the gap between a successful Hebrides or White Sands demonstration and a system that survives a sandstorm in the Empty Quarter. EOS's claim is that Apollo has been engineered specifically to operate inside these constraints in tactically useful ways. The Royal Netherlands Army's acceptance trials between 2026 and 2028 are the test of that claim.

> ## 4.4  Why Grizzly's Case Does Not Land
> 
> Grizzly's report is correctly read as a counterparty-quality short, not a fraud short. The claim that EOS faces material economic downside if Goldrone fails to perform is not what Grizzly demonstrated, and given how the contract is structured it is not what Grizzly could have demonstrated. The case runs through seven points.
> 
> First, the deposit is the gate. EOS does not commit production resources at the Singapore facility until Goldrone has paid US$18m in cash. If Goldrone never funds, EOS never starts. The worst outcome is a non-event. This is not a contract that obliges EOS to perform on receipt of a customer letter of intent. Second, the Letter of Credit transfers solvency risk to the issuing bank. A Korean-bank LC for approximately US$62m, once issued, is paid by the bank on conforming presentation of shipping and inspection documents, not by Goldrone. The LC requirement is precisely the mechanism EOS uses to neutralise Goldrone's modest balance sheet. Third, the facility inspection condition transfers technical acceptance risk to Goldrone. If Goldrone is not satisfied with EOS's manufacturing facility, the contract does not progress. EOS does not bear cost or reputational damage from a non-acceptance because it never built the unit.
> 
> Fourth, the contract is not in the unconditional order book. A$518m at 31 March 2026 is the figure on which EOS's revenue guidance and the Bell Potter, Canaccord Genuity and Ord Minnett valuations rest. The Goldrone US$80m sits explicitly outside it and was never relied upon for the FY26 break-even argument, which requires A$200m revenue from the existing backlog. Fifth, the Korean Joint Venture and IP licence are limited by design. EOS is obligated to provide a licence for the Korean market to the Joint Venture only subject to satisfaction of the conditions and obtaining relevant regulatory approvals. If the Korean Joint Venture never materialises because Goldrone never funds, EOS retains its 100 kW HELW intellectual property intact and can re-sell into Korea, through KORSAR, KAIST, the Agency for Defense Development, or another industrial partner, on better commercial terms.
> 
> Sixth, the UAE-Korea industrial agreement provides a higher-credit alternative path. EOS's 2 March 2026 announcement disclosed ongoing discussions with an established UAE manufacturing partner (Calidus) to develop proposals to support counter-drone requirements in the UAE and the Republic of Korea in connection with the US$35bn UAE-Korea defence industrial co-operation agreement. If Goldrone never converts, the underlying Korean demand may still be served through a Calidus-led or government-backed alternative. Seventh, the Korean defence procurement base rate is high. Hanwha, LIG Nex1, KAI and Hyundai Rotem have an essentially unblemished record of execution on multi-hundred-million-dollar export contracts to Australia, Poland, the UAE, Egypt, Saudi Arabia and Norway over the past decade. The relevant adverse base rate is for tiny Korean SPVs without a defence track record performing on export laser contracts, and that base rate is essentially unknown because the situation has no real precedent. The structural protections in the EOS contract are designed precisely to make Goldrone's track record irrelevant. If the conditions are met, EOS has a US$80m contract and a Korean industrial partner. If they are not, EOS has a Singapore facility, a Netherlands deliverable, and a Calidus-Korea industrial alternative.
> 
> ## 4.4  Why Schwer and the Board Signed It
> 
> The most plausible reading is that EOS treated Goldrone as a real-option vehicle for Korean market access, with downside fully bounded by the conditions precedent and upside that includes a Joint Venture, IP licensing royalties, and a Korean industrial footprint. The diligence EOS performed is implicit in the contract structure itself. A management team with real fraud exposure does not insist on US$18m cash in advance, an LC for the residual, and customer site acceptance, because those are precisely the conditions that prevent a fraudulent counterparty from extracting value. The contract reads as carefully designed to let EOS win if Goldrone performs and to lose almost nothing if Goldrone does not. The disclosure failing was that EOS did not place enough weight in the original 15 December announcement on the magnitude and significance of those conditions. That is a continuous-disclosure error, not a credit-risk error.
> 
> The disclosure track record is a real governance flag and gets the proper treatment in Part 4. Worth flagging here is the market's response to the 8 April court order, which sent the stock up roughly 24 percent in the following week. That is the empirical answer to how investors are weighting the legacy disclosure issues against the operational momentum.

That is the most caustic recorded sell-side line on the deal. Against it, three brokers maintain Buy ratings as of late April 2026. Bell Potter (Baxter Kirk) lifted the price target to A$10.40 from A$9.70 on 30 April 2026. Canaccord Genuity (Owen Humphries) sits at A$12.50. Ord Minnett (John Lawlor) is at A$12.95. Petra Capital (James Lennon) is the lone Hold at A$5.16 from August 2025, a target that pre-dates the laser contract conversions and is now substantially below the prevailing share price.

Unconditional contract backlog moved from A$459m at the end of 2025 to A$518m at quarter-end, a 13 percent quarter-on-quarter increase. The A$59m net rise understates the gross contract intake during the quarter, because backlog is also being burnt down by revenue recognised against existing orders as work gets delivered. > # 5.  Energy-Weapon Competitors
> 
> §1 set up the picture: only Israel's Iron Beam has crossed the line from demonstrator to operationally fielded HELW. This section narrows in on actual energy-weapon competitors, meaning the small set of companies that build laser weapons rather than the much wider universe of counter-UAS providers selling kinetic interceptors, electronic-warfare jammers, sensors, missiles or high-power microwave systems. The relevant peer set for Apollo is therefore narrower than a generic counter-drone competitor list. The subsections cover the United States, Europe, and Israel, in that order.
> 
> ## 5.1  The American Set
> 
> The American laser-weapon set has three layers. There are pure-play laser-module suppliers (nLight, General Atomics for some specialised modules), system-level integrators (Lockheed Martin, AeroVironment after its BlueHalo acquisition), and the legacy-prime laser efforts that remain in development phase (HII's Enduring HEL, residual Boeing and Northrop Grumman work). Apollo's competition with most of these is oblique rather than head-on, since the U.S. domestic market is closed off in practice to non-U.S. suppliers. The interesting overlap is for export customers in Europe, the Gulf, Asia-Pacific and Latin America, where the ITAR-free argument matters.
> 
> nLight, listed on NASDAQ as LASR, is the photonics pure-play that competes most directly with EOS at the laser-source level. The company has pivoted aggressively from industrial cutting and welding into Aerospace and Defense, now around 55 percent of total revenue, with A&D revenue up 60 percent year-on-year in 2025 to a record US$175m. The flagship directed-energy work is the HELSI-2 programme at US$171m total, a follow-on to a 300 kW class coherent-beam-combined laser delivered in 2023, targeting a one megawatt class output. nLight is also the laser-source supplier on the U.S. Army DE M-SHORAD programme through a US$34.5m subcontract to KORD Technologies. The company raised more than US$190m in additional capital in 2024 and 2025 to build a 50,000 square foot Colorado manufacturing facility. Its competitive positioning vis-à-vis EOS is upstream. nLight produces laser modules and amplifiers that go into other primes' weapons. EOS produces a complete weapon system with its own beam director, tracking and C2. The two companies could in principle co-exist as supplier and integrator, but EOS's architectural choice to source non-U.S. fibre modules makes that co-existence unlikely in practice.
> 
> AeroVironment, listed on NASDAQ as AVAV, completed its US$4.1bn all-stock acquisition of BlueHalo in May 2025 and now owns the LOCUST family of laser counter-drone systems. LOCUST is a 20 kW class laser. The first units were delivered to the U.S. Army under the AMP-HEL prototyping contract, originally awarded to BlueHalo for US$45.7m in April 2023. The first two LOCUST systems were mounted on GM Defense Infantry Squad Vehicles and accepted by the Army in September 2025. The next two on Oshkosh JLTVs were delivered in December 2025. The LOCUST X3 unveiled at the AUSA Global Force Symposium in March 2026 lifted output to 20 to 35-plus kW with explicit design against Group 1 to 3 UAS including Iranian Shahed and Russian Geran-class drones. The 12 February 2026 incident in which a LOCUST-equipped Infantry Squad Vehicle was reportedly fired without sufficient FAA coordination, prompting an eight-hour shutdown of El Paso airspace, was the first publicly disclosed operational use of a U.S. counter-drone laser. AVAV competes with Apollo at the lower end of the power spectrum. LOCUST is a 20 to 35 kW system to Apollo's 100 to 150 kW. But it is operationally fielded with the U.S. Army, which Apollo is not.
> 
> Lockheed Martin's directed-energy portfolio is the most diverse of the U.S. primes. HELIOS, the 60-plus kW shipboard system installed on USS Preble in 2022 and scalable to 120 kW, is the operational reference point. DEIMOS, a 50 kW Stryker-mounted system, achieved first light in 2026. ATHENA at 30 kW remains the baseline counter-UAV demonstrator. AHEL is the airborne variant, now in flight test on AC-130J. The Rafael partnership to scale Iron Beam to 300 kW introduces ITAR exposure on the Israeli side that would otherwise constrain Iron Beam exports. Lockheed's approach is integration into existing combat systems (Aegis on HELIOS) rather than greenfield design. EOS does not compete with Lockheed directly because Lockheed sells to U.S. and Five-Eyes customers, and EOS does not. The two companies are likely to encounter each other only in third-country competitions where ITAR is the decisive variable.
> 
> Two other U.S. names matter at the margin. Northrop Grumman has legacy chemical-laser heritage from the SDI era and continues to do directed-energy work at lower visibility, with the Skyguard chemical laser the most recognised legacy product, and a relationship with EOS as the buyer of Slinger systems for the Northrop Grumman Agnostic Gun Truck. General Atomics has demonstrated airborne laser pods that have not yet converted to a programme of record. HII's Enduring HEL Phase 2 facility opened in September 2025 to host the U.S. Army's revised laser road-map after the IFPC-HEL programme was narrowed from four prototypes to one and DE M-SHORAD was redirected. Boeing HEL-MD and Kratos's LaWS sub-system contributions are historic milestones rather than current competitive threats. None of these companies has an export franchise in lasers that overlaps with Apollo's positioning.
> 
> ## 5.2  The European Set
> 
> The principal European laser-weapon programme is the UK DragonFire, run by an MBDA UK, Leonardo UK, QinetiQ and Dstl consortium. DragonFire is a 50 kW class fibre-combined laser. Successive trials at the Hebrides Range in 2024 and 2025 demonstrated above-the-horizon engagement of drones at speeds up to 650 km per hour. The UK Ministry of Defence signed a £316m production contract with MBDA in November 2025 for two units, followed by a £67m QinetiQ laser-source contract in January 2026. Defence Minister Vernon Coaker confirmed in March 2026 that the first system would be installed on a Type 45 destroyer in 2027, five years ahead of the original 2032 timetable. The official cost per shot is £10. Within the consortium MBDA is the prime, QinetiQ supplies the laser source, Leonardo provides the beam director, and Dstl is the customer's technical authority. DragonFire is the most operationally credible non-Israeli laser weapon in Europe, and the only European programme that has both a production contract and a hard fielding date. It is also ITAR-constrained outside the Five Eyes, which means EOS rather than the DragonFire consortium is the realistic bidder into Germany, the Netherlands, France, Italy, Turkey and the Gulf.
> 
> On the German side, the Rheinmetall and MBDA Deutschland ARGE produced the Sachsen demonstrator described in §1.3, a 20 kW spectral-beam-combined laser in a 20-foot ISO container that conducted more than one hundred live firings across six campaigns from the F124 frigate Sachsen. The demonstrator was transferred to WTD 91 at Meppen in October 2025 for land-based qualification. An operational German Navy laser is targeted for 2029, with scaling to a 100 kW class. Rheinmetall is also developing a Skyranger 30 HEL variant on the same upstream technology base, initially at 20 kW with a road-map to 50 kW and an "ideal goal" of 100 kW. The Skyranger 30 HEL pathway is the most likely European competitor to Apollo at the higher power band over the second half of this decade, though it remains at concept stage with no signed export contract.
> 
> France runs a national counter-UAS laser programme through the DGA, with the consortium of Thales, MBDA, Safran and Cilas as the principal industrial team. The programme has not produced a fielded system as of mid-2026 and runs at lower public profile than DragonFire or the Sachsen demonstrator. The European Sky Shield Initiative is the framework procurement vehicle that binds much of the European demand picture, and the SAFE and EDIS rules requiring half of EU procurement from EU-domiciled suppliers by 2030 are the principal reason EOS is publicly considering an EU re-domicile to Germany or the Netherlands. The European laser map, summarised: DragonFire is real but ITAR-constrained beyond Five Eyes; the German programme is at least four years behind Apollo on operational readiness; the French programme is further behind. Apollo is the only system in the European theatre that is both ITAR-free and ready for export delivery in 2026.
> 
> ## 5.3  The Israeli Set
> 
> Rafael is the central Israeli reference point. Iron Beam at 100 kW with a range of up to seven kilometres in clear conditions, falling sharply in fog or dust, with a cost per shot officially less than the price of a hot dog in New York (approximately US$3.50 marginal, approximately US$2,000 amortised), was declared operational by the Israeli Ministry of Defense in mid-September 2025 with first Air Force handover on 28 December 2025. The full system saw its first publicly described combat engagement on 2 March 2026, intercepting rockets. The total Israeli production deal has been reported at NIS 2bn, approximately US$625m. Lower-power lasers in the same family shot down approximately forty Hezbollah drones in October 2024. During the forty-day Iran conflict starting February 2026, a version of Iron Beam was reportedly transferred to the UAE alongside Iron Dome and a Spectro surveillance system. The Times of Israel and Jerusalem Post note that Iron Beam itself saw only limited Iran-war use due to a small number of fielded batteries. The Naval Iron Beam variant is in development for Reshef-class corvettes. The 50 kW Iron Beam-M mobile variant is being developed on a Tatra chassis. The 10 kW Lite Beam is in development for larger infantry vehicles. Rafael's partnership with Lockheed Martin on the 300 kW Iron Beam upgrade introduces ITAR exposure that has produced zero publicly confirmed Iron Beam exports outside Israel as of May 2026.
> 
> Elbit Systems is the second Israeli participant in the laser map, supplying core laser-source elements for Iron Beam and Lite Beam and developing laser-based airborne UAS hard-kill demonstrators on the Hermes 900 platform. Israel Aerospace Industries does not have a fielded HEL of its own but was EOS's local partner in the comparative shoot-off in Israel that Schwer has referenced publicly, where the EOS and IAI combination reportedly outperformed two internationally-renowned local Israeli competitors. The Israeli laser ecosystem is small and tightly held, with Rafael and Elbit dominant and the export pipeline mediated through the Israeli MoD. Rafael's Drone Dome, Spike, Python, the Elbit SMASH and ReDrone systems, IAI's Drone Guard and Mini Harpy, and Smart Shooter's small-arms sights are all part of the wider Israeli counter-UAS landscape, but they sit outside the laser-weapon scope of this section.
> 
> The Israeli set is where the geo-strategic stakes for EOS are highest. Iron Beam has the only operational kill record of any 100 kW HELW in service. Apollo has the only ITAR-free 100 kW export contract. The Calidus relationship and the disclosed UAE-Korea industrial co-operation agreement are EOS's counter to Israeli incumbency in the Gulf, with the additional argument that Apollo can be transferred without U.S. or Israeli political vetoes. That is the single most important commercial argument EOS makes in the region.
> 
> ## 5.4 Comparative Positioning
> 
> Apollo's edge in this comparison is structural rather than performance-based. The system is exportable in weeks under the Australian Defence Strategic Goods List rather than years under U.S. ITAR. It is deployment-ready at 100 kW in 2026, where no other 100 kW non-Israeli system has a signed export contract. The IP is fully owned by EOS, allowing localisation in the customer country. The full stack, sensors, beam director, tracking, adaptive optics and C2, is under EOS control. Pricing is competitive at €30m to €50m per unit at scale. Dwell time and slew rate are demonstrated. From the Singapore facility opening in February 2026 onward, the production capacity is real. Where Apollo is at risk is operational. Iron Beam's combat record cannot be matched. The roughly forty Hezbollah drone kills and operational integration into Iron Dome are facts that no demonstration can equal. The group-level financial scale against Lockheed at US$74bn FY24 revenue is two orders of magnitude lower. U.S. domestic market access is closed off in practice for non-U.S. suppliers, with the AeroVironment-BlueHalo LOCUST line filling the lower-power Army slot. The lab-to-field transition risk Congress flagged for DE M-SHORAD and 300 kW IFPC-HEL is the same engineering risk Apollo faces. Whether EOS has engineered around it is what the Royal Netherlands Army acceptance trials between 2026 and 2028 will answer.
> 
> # 6.  The MARSS Acquisitions
> 
> Part 1 introduced MARSS as a Europe-based defence and security technology specialist with offices in London, Bristol, Monaco and Riyadh. This section walks through what MARSS actually does, why EOS bought it, and how the combined stack fits together. The structural argument is that the two MARSS legs convert EOS from a best-of-breed component supplier into something closer to an integrated counter-drone systems prime, able to bid on programmes that the gun-RWS plus laser proposition could not credibly approach.
> 
> ## 6.1  The Company. Founded 2005 by Johannes Pinl
> 
> MARSS Group is a privately held defence and security technology firm founded in 2005 by Johannes Pinl, the current CEO and President, originally as the developer of an autonomous vessel docking concept. The 2005 founding date is consistent across MARSS's own publications, the Tracxn corporate database and Pinl's own statements. The EOS press materials around the November 2025 Interceptor acquisition use 2006, which appears to be an inadvertent simplification. As of mid-2024 MARSS had ninety-four employees according to Tracxn, distributed across offices in Monaco (the headquarters), London, Bristol and Riyadh, with smaller presences in the United States in Arlington, Virginia and Arizona. RocketReach reports MARSS Group revenue of approximately US$15.9m in 2026. EOS's own diligence on the wider group identified historical revenue of approximately €243m between 2020 and 2025, with the discrepancy explained by the group structure. The UK subsidiary that Grizzly Research focused on captures only a portion of group revenue, with the Monaco and Saudi entities holding the balance.
> 
> The product line has four pillars. The first is the NiDAR Core IoT platform, an AI-powered command-and-control system that fuses radar, electro-optical, infrared, RF, sonar and other sensors into a single 360-degree situational picture. NiDAR is deployed at more than sixty sites globally per MARSS marketing, including yachts, prisons, oil platforms, ports, military bases and palaces. It is interoperable under the UK MoD SAPIENT standard. Variants include NiDAR 4D, NiDAR Command, NiDAR X and NiDAR CUAS. The second is MOBtronic, a man-overboard detection system for ships and offshore platforms. The third is RADiRguard, a high-security perimeter surveillance system, deployed in a US$5.5m, up-to-forty-unit Middle Eastern critical-infrastructure contract disclosed in 2024. The fourth is the Interceptor family of AI-guided hard-kill interceptor drones treated in §6.2 below.
> 
> The MARSS contract pipeline as disclosed includes a multi-year programme with Nigeria for an AI-powered shield protecting critical infrastructure and borders (one of the largest national defence programmes commissioned in Africa), a US$20m-plus naval combat vessel deployment with a South-East Asian customer, a UK Export Finance-supported African expeditionary C2 platform contract, the UAE's DAMITA fully indigenous integrated counter-drone and air-defence programme, and pre-existing relationships with the U.S. CURRENT counter-UAS exercise series. The BlueHalo and MARSS strategic partnership announced in 2024 is now effectively dissolved by AeroVironment's acquisition of BlueHalo and EOS's acquisition of MARSS, a non-trivial competitive event since it removes any near-term scope for AVAV plus NiDAR integration.
> 
> ## 6.2 The Interceptor-MR and Interceptor-SR
> 
> The unit economics are the commercial premise of the system. A traditional short-range air-defence missile costs roughly US$150,000 to US$200,000 per shot. The Interceptor-MR's marginal cost is around one-fifth of that, and the system is reusable in routine engagements. Against a fleet of Iranian Shahed drones at low tens of thousands of dollars apiece, the Interceptor-MR is the first kinetic counter-UAS system that is cost-positive in routine use. The reusability mechanism is unusual enough to be worth describing. The motors stop and the blades fold immediately before impact. The drone rams the target and recovers either by vertical landing or by deploying a rear parachute. Battle damage to the Interceptor-MR itself is repaired through modular replacement of nose, tail or wings. The 90 percent single-pass hit probability is a manufacturer claim that has not been independently validated. The down-selection by a NATO member state, which MARSS has not publicly identified, is the closest the system has come to external validation. The 50 percent expansion of Bristol Defence Labs in 2025 is the kind of production scaling that does not happen without serious customer interest.
> 
> ## 6.3  The Two Acquisition Tranches
> 
> EOS structured the MARSS acquisition in two tranches, eight weeks apart, with the Interceptor business carved out first and the rest of the group acquired second. The structural reason for the two-step approach is that the Interceptor business was the most operationally urgent piece, the one EOS wanted to integrate with the existing R-family RWS line as quickly as possible, and the one with the cleanest valuation profile. The wider MARSS group brought in NiDAR and the broader customer base, both of which required more careful diligence and earnout structure.
> 
> The first tranche, announced 19 November 2025, acquired the MARSS Interceptor business for €5.5m, approximately A$10m, up front, with an additional EOS investment commitment of up to A$10m over three years for further development and potential customer development funding. The contract documents anticipate twelve to twenty-four months of further development before full commercial launch. EOS acquires all of the Interceptor assets from MARSS and employs the team of specialised engineers that built the product. Funding came from existing EOS cash reserves with no material near-term financial impact.
> 
> The second tranche, announced 12 January 2026, acquired the rest of the MARSS group for US$36m up front, approximately A$54m, funded from existing cash reserves of approximately A$107m at 31 December 2025. The earnout runs to up to €100m, approximately A$174m, tied to new MARSS third-party contract orders. The earnout pays up to €20m for each €100m or part thereof of qualifying new orders, capped at €500m of orders covered by the earnout. Earnout is payable in a mix of cash, capped at €20m, and EOS shares, with the milestone period ending no later than 31 May 2027. The structure is an asset acquisition covering NiDAR C2 technology, the sensor-fusion and AI software platform, hardware, customer contracts, intellectual property and personnel. Earnings impact is broadly neutral to earnings and operating cash flow in 2026, with positive contribution expected from 2027. EOS funded the transaction with a US$100m two-year secured committed term loan facility, the first material debt the company has carried since the Washington H. Soul Pattinson loan was repaid in January 2025.
> 
> The earnout structure is the critical commercial signal. EOS is paying a relatively modest US$36m up front for a business that, on its own account, has done €243m of cumulative revenue over five years, but is willing to pay up to €100m more if MARSS continues to grow new contract orders at a meaningful pace. The structure transfers growth-execution risk to the seller, with Pinl and the existing MARSS team retained as employees, and protects EOS shareholders from over-paying. Bell Potter analyst Baxter Kirk's January 2026 note explicitly modelled no earnout-period conditions being achieved as the conservative case, lifting EOS's price target to A$12.00 from A$9.00 even on that assumption, and subsequently moderating to A$9.70 in February and back to A$10.40 in late April after additional contract data.
> 
> ## 6.4  Strategic Logic. From Component Supplier to Systems Prime
> 
> EOS's stated rationale, repeated across the January press release, the Bell Potter note, and the post-Singapore investor materials, is the conversion from a best-of-breed effector and sensor supplier into an integrated counter-drone systems provider, capable of bidding for larger, higher-value programmes as a prime contractor. The framing includes turn-key delivery and operation of critical-infrastructure protection in military, homeland security and civil markets such as airports and power plants. Embedding NiDAR into the EOS R-family remote weapon systems creates the mesh-networked vehicle fleet vision Schwer described in February 2026. Each vehicle in a customer's fleet, equipped with an EOS RWS plus integrated NiDAR sensors and effector control, becomes a node in a hemispherical air-defence shield. Detection, classification, prioritisation, effector selection (Slinger gun for swarms at moderate range, Apollo laser for Group 1 to 3 UAS at longer range and lower per-shot cost, Interceptor-MR for higher-value or harder targets), engagement decision under operator supervision, and battle-damage assessment are all performed within the NiDAR fabric. The architectural endpoint, in EOS's framing, is a counter-drone stack functionally equivalent to Anduril's Lattice plus Anvil plus Roadrunner plus Pulsar combination, but ITAR-free and EU-attractive.
> 
> ## 6.5  The Integrated Stack in a Deployed Scenario
> 
> Picture the Royal Netherlands Army's Apollo unit protecting a forward base alongside a Skyranger 30 battery under the European Sky Shield architecture, or a Saudi critical-infrastructure site running on the existing MARSS NiDAR contracts. NiDAR runs the show. Radar (the SPEXER 2000M MkIII or comparable AESA), electro-optical and infrared, RF and acoustic feeds all fuse into a single integrated air picture. AI imaging classifies what is coming in, telling a Group 1 quadcopter from a Group 2 fixed-wing, a Shahed-class one-way attack drone, a Geran, or a cruise missile, and computes engagement priority. Then it picks the cheapest effector that will work for each track. A single Group 1 drone inside one kilometre routes to Apollo at sub-dollar marginal cost. A drone swarm inside Skyranger range routes to the 30 mm AHEAD round at US$100 to US$1,000 an engagement. A hardened or higher-value threat routes to the Interceptor-MR at US$30,000 to US$40,000, or, only if it has to, to a CAMM or IRIS-T or Aster missile at much higher cost. The operator validates the engagement, sensor fusion compresses the decision timeline to seconds, and the EO/IR system confirms the kill. Apollo returns to standby with effectively zero ammunition expended. The Interceptor-MR returns to base or parachutes down for refurbishment.
> 
> The mesh-network innovation that EOS has emphasised is that this entire sequence can run across a fleet of vehicles, each carrying a partial NiDAR node, with hemispherical coverage even when individual vehicles are obscured by terrain. No competitor, not Rheinmetall, not Anduril, not Rafael, currently offers this exact stack on an ITAR-free basis with a 100 kW laser as the long-range hard-kill option. The Riyadh office is why EOS chose to announce the ROKETSAN partnership at the Riyadh World Defense Show in February 2026 rather than at IDEX or DSEI. With the MARSS Saudi presence joining the EOS Slinger and R-family and Apollo and Calidus footprint, EOS is putting together an integrated Gulf delivery platform that competes directly with Israeli incumbents on technology and with U.S. primes on price and ITAR-freedom.

> # 7.  Caveats
> 
> A handful of things to keep in mind before treating any of the above as conviction.
> 
> Most of EOS's public positioning on Apollo is company marketing. The twenty-to-thirty drones-per-minute engagement rate, the "never lost a live-fire exercise" line, and the claim that EOS is the only company worldwide that can offer 100 kW systems and manufacture them locally are EOS statements, not independent test results. The Royal Netherlands Army's purchase does not contradict them, but it does not validate them either. The lab-to-field gap is the related risk. Even well-funded U.S. laser programmes have struggled to carry clean range performance into dirty, dusty, vibrating tactical conditions, which is why DE M-SHORAD and IFPC-HEL were both downgraded in March 2026. Apollo's 34 kW Australian field demonstration is encouraging, not conclusive. The Royal Netherlands Army acceptance trials between 2026 and 2028, plus the first customer engagement of the Singapore-built 100 kW unit, are the tests that matter.
> 
> The Goldrone contract may simply not convert. The conditions precedent have already slipped from January to Q2 2026, and Bell Potter's 30 April note is explicit that there is no certainty conversion will happen. The argument against Grizzly was never that the contract would fund. It was that EOS's downside if it does not fund is bounded.
> 
> The disclosure track record is a real governance flag. The A$4m Federal Court penalty in April 2026, the ASX's criticism of the December 2025 Goldrone announcement, the revised continuous disclosure policy in March, and the separate ASIC action against former CEO Dr Ben Greene all sit in the same cluster. None of them establishes misrepresentation. Cumulatively, they raise reasonable questions about how disciplined EOS is around market-sensitive information. Part 4 takes that thread up properly.
> 
> Iron Beam is the benchmark Apollo cannot beat on combat record. Roughly forty Hezbollah drone kills in October 2024, the operational declaration in September 2025, the Air Force handover in December, and the first publicly described Iron Beam engagement on 2 March 2026 give Rafael the only kill log against real hostile drones in the category. Apollo will not match that record until a customer state uses it in anger. Schwer's pitch, that Apollo's edge is operational availability today, ITAR-freedom, IP ownership and exportability rather than combat pedigree, does not pretend the gap is not there. Gulf customers in particular will weigh the Iron Beam record heavily, even as the sovereignty argument for an Australian alternative cuts the other way.
> 
> These caveats sit inside the wider four-part picture. Part 1 worked through the Remote Weapon System franchise that still pays the bills. This part has been about HELW counter-drone. Part 3 turns to the other end of EOS's directed-energy work, the space business: satellite laser ranging, orbital-debris tracking, and the anti-satellite mission that the same Apollo-class beam directors and adaptive optics underpin. Part 4 is the Schwer governance chapter that pulls the three operational stories back to a unified investment view of the equity.
