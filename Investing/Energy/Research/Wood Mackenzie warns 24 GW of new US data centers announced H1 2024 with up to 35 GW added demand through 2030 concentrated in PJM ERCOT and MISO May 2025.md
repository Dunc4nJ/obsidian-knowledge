# Wood Mackenzie warns 24 GW of new US data centers announced H1 2024 with up to 35 GW added demand through 2030 concentrated in PJM, ERCOT, and MISO

## Key Takeaways

- **Demand context**: US power demand was largely flat for 20 years but EIA projects 4,082 → 4,179 billion kWh from 2024 → 2025, with Wood Mackenzie's house view calling 2-3% per year through 2030 (reaching 5,178 billion kWh by 2050). Data centers are cited as a primary driver of this break from decades of stagnation, alongside transport electrification, heating/cooling, industrial processes, and potential green hydrogen — meaning grid expansion bottlenecks compete across multiple end-markets, not just AI.

- **AI data center sizing**: 24 GW of new US data centers were announced in H1 2024 alone (more than triple the H1 2023 figure), and total 2025 investment is projected to be double 2023. Big-four hyperscaler (Amazon, Meta, Google, Microsoft) capex is forecast to rise 16% per year through 2025. Analyst estimates for data-center electricity demand growth span 10-20% per year through 2030 — at the high end this implies **35 GW of added demand**. Hyperscale (≥1 GW or 1 million sq ft) facilities are becoming increasingly common.

- **Grid concentration creates asymmetric pricing**: Siting clusters in PJM (Northern Virginia), ERCOT (Dallas-Fort Worth), and SPP/MISO (northern Midwest) strain already-overloaded local grids. Wood Mackenzie's proprietary Security Constrained Economic Dispatch (SCED) model finds incremental announced large loads could push real-time prices up by **US$1 in NYISO to nearly US$70 in PJM** — the dispersion itself is the investment signal: behind-the-meter generation makes most economic sense where the locational marginal price spread is largest.

- **Reliability gap is structural, not cyclical**: NERC's 2024 Long-Term Reliability Assessment flags ~half of the US as facing shortfall risk in the next decade due to insufficient dispatchable capacity. Gartner predicts 40% of existing global data centers will be operationally power-constrained by 2027. Power issues are already the most common cause of severe data-center outages, with >50% of significant incidents costing >US$100K and 16% costing >US$1M (Uptime Institute) — translating directly into willingness-to-pay for behind-the-meter solutions like [[Bloom Energy (BE)]] fuel cells, [[GE Vernova (GEV)]] aeroderivative turbines, [[Capstone Power+ (CGEH)]] microturbines, and [[Solaris Energy Infrastructure (SEI)]] mobile power.

- **Round-the-clock requirement vs. ESG renewables tension**: Most data centers operate 24/7 and require resilient, always-on power. The article notes the awkward fit with renewables-only PPA structures that big tech currently prefers from an ESG standpoint, and flags an emerging operational risk — large data centers automatically disconnecting during normal grid faults — that creates IBR-like (inverter-based-resource) intermittency from the demand side. This is a tailwind for firm-power vendors and an edge case the firm-power thesis hasn't fully priced.

- **Article scope caveat**: this is a 5-minute partnered-content piece dated 7 May 2025, NOT an updated 2026 forecast. The headline figures are 24 GW announced H1 2024 and up to 35 GW added by 2030 — it does NOT contain a "90 GW total / 59 GW data centers / 21% upward revision" framing. Its in-text "related content" link references a separate Energy Gang podcast claiming 220 GW of additional data-center demand, which is a distinct (and much larger) number sourced elsewhere. Treat this note as the primary-source baseline for Wood Mac's mid-2025 view; later forecast revisions live in separate notes.

> # What the data center boom means for grids
>
> The artificial intelligence boom will generate huge demand for reliable, round-the-clock electricity at scale – and grids must adapt
>
> 07 May 2025
>
> 5 minute read
>
> ## The context
>
> Electricity demand in developed countries has been largely stable for decades, but the energy transition will drive a massive increase in electricity demand to 2050. Having stayed relatively flat for [20 years](https://www.eia.gov/totalenergy/data/monthly/pdf/sec7_19.pdf), the Energy Information Administration projects power demand in the US will rise from a record 4,082 billion kWh in 2024 to 4,179 billion kilowatt hours (kWh) in 2025, and Wood Mackenzie expects it to continue to increase by 2-3% per year through 2030. By 2050, it is predicted to hit 5,178 billion [kWh](https://www.eia.gov/outlooks/aeo/data/browser/#/?id=8-AEO2023&cases=ref2023&sourcekey=0).
>
> At the same time, as more distributed energy resources come online, grids must adapt to serve as multidirectional distribution hosts for customers who will increasingly supply electricity to the grid as well as taking power from it. As the leader in grid transformation, [S&C Electric Company](https://www.sandc.com/en/solutions/data-centers/?utm_campaign=pandr-amer-sc-articles&utm_medium=display&utm_source=client-portal&utm_content=sc-article-1) is well-placed to assess the implications for the power sector of this step change in the size and nature of resource required, and outline how the grid must adapt to address it.
>
> ## A significant new source of demand
>
> Cloud computing and industrial scale crypto mining have made data centers a notable new source of electricity demand, but with the rapid commercialization of generative artificial intelligence (AI), this demand will be turbocharged over the next decade. However, while big tech firms are used to a 'move fast and break things' ethos, the power sector works in timeframes of five-to-ten years, making access to electricity a major obstacle to growth. Data centers will also be competing with other new sources of demand for electricity, particularly transport, heating and cooling but also industrial processes and potentially green hydrogen.
>
> ## The scale of the challenge
>
> As a new and rapidly evolving commercial technology, estimates vary widely on the potential impact of AI in the US, although few doubt that it will be hugely significant. Having established the American AI Initiative via Executive Order in his [first term](https://trumpwhitehouse.archives.gov/ai/executive-order-ai/), one of President Trump's first actions on returning to power was an executive order aimed at removing barriers to US leadership in [AI](https://www.whitehouse.gov/presidential-actions/2025/01/removing-barriers-to-american-leadership-in-artificial-intelligence/).
>
> With most of the big global tech firms based in the United States, a significant percentage of the world's data is stored in the country. Wood Mackenzie estimates that the US already hosts up to 40% of total global data center capacity, a market worth over US$68 billion in [2024](https://www.globenewswire.com/news-release/2024/9/18/2948393/0/en/Data-Center-Market-Size-Expected-to-Reach-USD-775-73-Billion-by-2034.html). As AI adds significantly to demand, data center capacity is almost certain to escalate [significantly](https://www.woodmac.com/link/a247956f5fd440f99b3af6298063b9a0.aspx). New data centers requiring almost 24GW were announced in the first half of 2024, more than triple the announcements for the same period in 2023, and overall 2025 investment is set to be double that seen in [2023](https://www.woodmac.com/link/e5fa5c0c1fee45d9898787255421d624.aspx).
>
> The big four tech firms (Amazon, Meta, Google and Microsoft) forecast annual capex increases of 16% per year through 2025, while analysts' forecasts for electricity demand growth from data centers range from 10-20% per year through 2030. At the top end of these demand growth estimates, 35 gigawatts (GW) of demand would be [added](https://www.woodmac.com/link/a247956f5fd440f99b3af6298063b9a0.aspx). Overall, there seems little doubt that data centers will drive a large proportion of electricity demand growth through to the end of the decade.
>
> ## The shape of the problem
>
> Siting decisions for large data centers tend to focus on existing hubs within Independent System Operator (ISO) regions, further straining already overloaded local grids. Growing concentrations of large load within ISOs in locations such as Northern Virginia in PJM, Dallas-Fort Worth in ERCOT and the northern Midwest in SPP/MISO are likely to result in a highly asymmetrical power market. Using a proprietary Security Constrained Economic Dispatch (SCED) model to forecast real-time (RT) electricity prices, Wood Mackenzie analysts found that additional announced large loads could result in RT price increases of anywhere from US$1 in NYISO to almost US$70 in [PJM](https://www.woodmac.com/link/a2b43513c7394321b53c8110e0bc061a.aspx). To complicate matters, data centers are also getting bigger, with hyperscale facilities (over one gigawatt (GW) or one million square feet in size) becoming increasingly common.
>
> A further issue is that data centers need access to reliable, resilient, round-the-clock sources of electricity; creating potential issues if facilities rely on power from renewables – which many tech firms currently prefer from an ESG standpoint. As S&C points out, the challenges data centers create for the grid therefore relate not just to surging demand, but to the added uncertainty created by adding a concentration of large loads to a system, particularly in terms of forecasting peak and hourly loads. To operate efficiently, data centers require significant heating and cooling – American Society of Heating, Refrigerating and Air-Conditioning Engineers (ASHRAE) guidelines recommend that commercial scale facilities be kept within a temperature range of [18-27o C](https://www.ashrae.org/file%20library/technical%20resources/bookstore/supplemental%20files/therm-gdlns-5th-r-e-refcard.pdf).
>
> Most data centers operate around the clock. However, facilities dedicated to tasks such as crypto mining that are not time critical can scale their activity levels up and down based on intraday electricity prices, leading to significant variability in demand. Another emerging issue is that of large data centers automatically disconnecting during normal grid faults. This can lead to operating issues similar to those caused by the inherent intermittency of inverter-based resources (IBRs) such as wind and solar.
>
> ## A growing issue
>
> Power issues are consistently the most common cause of serious and severe data center outages – and they are likely to get worse. In its latest Long-Term Reliability Assessment, the North American Electric Reliability Corporation (NERC) found that lower than needed dispatchable capacity points to 'critical reliability challenges' facing the industry. It concluded that around half of the United States is at risk of shortfalls that could cause reduced power supply and outages in the next [decade](https://www.nerc.com/pa/RAPA/ra/Reliability%20Assessments%20DL/NERC_Long%20Term%20Reliability%20Assessment_2024.pdf). According to the US Energy Information Administration, states with the biggest issues in terms of both frequency and duration include Maine, West Virginia and [Vermont](https://www.eia.gov/todayinenergy/detail.php?id=61303). Meanwhile, in a separate study looking specifically at data centers, Gartner predicted that 40% of existing facilities globally will be operationally constrained by access to sufficient power by [2027](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2024-11-12-gartner-predicts-power-shortages-will-restrict-40-percent-of-ai-data-centers-by-20270).
>
> ## What happens if a data center goes down?
>
> Outages are extremely costly for data centers. Research by the Uptime Institute found that more than half of significant incidents cost more than $100,000, and 16% costing more than [$1 million](https://uptimeinstitute.com/resources/research-and-reports/annual-outage-analysis-2024). Generally, they lead to downtime, loss of revenue and additional costs, as well as significant stress and the need for additional resource to address the issue. As data centers are increasingly used for AI applications, the uncertainty and disruption caused could become even more serious due to the opaque nature of AI algorithms.
