Mobile manufacturing on a customer campus (OpenAI-style) is on the table.

> # SECTION 4: THE ABSORPTION CHILLER BREAKTHROUGH — CGEH'S ENERGY SURPLUS PROGRAM (ESP)
>
> How CGEH's Absorption Chiller Technology Breaks Away from All Competing Solutions
>
> Of all the competitive differentiators in CGEH's technology portfolio, the Energy Surplus Program (ESP) — specifically the integration of absorption chillers with microturbine waste heat recovery — is the most structurally important and least appreciated by the investor community. This technology is not merely an ancillary feature; it is a paradigm-shifting capability that dramatically alters the economic calculus for data center power procurement and positions CGEH as uniquely superior to any other BTM power solution currently available.
>
> Understanding the Technology: Electric Chillers vs. Absorption Chillers
>
> In a conventional data center, roughly 30-40% of total facility power consumption is devoted to cooling — specifically, keeping GPU and CPU servers within safe operating temperature ranges. Today's data centers universally employ electric chillers, which require dedicated electrical power to operate. In a large hyperscale data center, this means hundreds of megawatts of electrical capacity are consumed simply to cool the servers, not to perform any compute work.
>
> CGEH's microturbines, as part of their normal power generation cycle, produce significant quantities of high-grade waste heat. Rather than dissipating this heat (as most power systems do), CGEH's ESP harvests this waste heat and feeds it into an Absorption Chiller system. Absorption chillers use thermal energy — heat — rather than electrical energy to drive the refrigeration cycle. The practical result: the same fuel that generates the data center's electricity also, simultaneously and without any additional fuel cost, generates the cooling the data center requires.

> # SECTION 5: AIR BEARING TECHNOLOGY — ZERO OIL, ONE MOVING PART, SUPERIOR RELIABILITY

> The Engineering Advantage That Becomes More Valuable as Data Centers Age
>
> CGEH's air bearing microturbine system is among the most misunderstood and underappreciated technology differentiators in the distributed power sector. While investors focus on topline revenue growth and data center deal pipelines, the long-term operational superiority of CGEH's air bearing design is a durable, structural advantage that will only become more valuable over time as data center infrastructure ages and maintenance costs accumulate across competing technology installations.
>
> How Air Bearings Work — And Why They Matter
>
> Traditional reciprocating engines (gas generators, diesel gensets) and many gas microturbine designs require physical contact between moving parts, necessitating lubricating oils, filters, coolants, and complex maintenance schedules. CGEH's proprietary air bearing technology replaces all of this with a thin film of pressurized air that creates a virtually frictionless operating environment for the single rotating assembly (the turbine rotor), which spins at approximately 60,000 RPM in the C200 / C1000 platform (96,000 RPM in the C65). For scale: most cars redline below 7,000 RPM and Ferrari exotic cars cap near 10,000 RPM.
>

> MTBF and the Mission-Critical Data Center Calculus
>

In a mission-critical data center, even a brief power interruption can result in hundreds of millions of dollars in lost revenue, data corruption, SLA penalties, and customer churn. For hyperscalers like Amazon Web Services, Microsoft Azure, and Google Cloud, power uptime is non-negotiable. The reliability calculus for selecting a behind-the-meter power solution is therefore primarily driven by MTBF, not first-cost. On this dimension, CGEH's air bearing microturbines are class-leading:

> - CGEH has units in the field operating for 20–25 years with no major component replacements — a testament to air bearing durability.

>
> - Field-demonstrated MTBF exceeds 40,000 hours — more than 4.5 years of continuous operation before the first expected maintenance event.
>
> - No oil changes, no coolant flushes, no filter replacements — maintenance is simplified to remote monitoring plus infrequent on-site service visits.
>
> - The C65 model replaced at the City of Janesville, Wisconsin wastewater plant in 2025 had operated for over 15 years and logged more than 100,000 hours of runtime.

> # SECTION 6: 800 VOLT DIRECT CURRENT (800VDC) — THE ARCHITECTURE STANDARD THAT MAKES CGEH ESSENTIAL
>
> Why 800VDC Is Becoming the Mandatory Standard for AI Data Centers
>
> The transition of data center power architecture from traditional medium-voltage alternating current (specifically 415V AC) to 800 Volt Direct Current (800VDC) represents one of the most significant infrastructure paradigm shifts in the technology industry in decades. This is not a "nice-to-have" optimization; it is an engineering necessity driven by the extreme power density demands of NVIDIA's latest GPU architectures — Blackwell, Vera Rubin, Kyber, and Rubin Ultra — which require power delivery at densities that 415V AC infrastructure simply cannot support economically or physically. Following NVIDIA's GTC 2026 conference, 800VDC has moved from prototype to production-ready reference designs, and the standard is being codified for gigawatt-scale AI factories.
>
> The Technical and Economic Case for 800VDC
>
> At a fundamental level, electrical power equals voltage multiplied by current (P = V × I). For a fixed power delivery requirement, higher voltage means proportionally lower current. Lower current means dramatically reduced resistive losses (which scale with the square of current: P_loss = I² × R), smaller conductor cross-sections, and lower thermal dissipation throughout the power delivery chain. For AI data centers delivering megawatts of power to GPU racks, these efficiency gains compound to enormous scale.
>
> Comparative analysis: delivering 1 MW at 48VDC requires approximately 20,800 amperes of current. The same 1 MW at 800VDC requires only 1,250 amperes — a 16x reduction in current and therefore a massive reduction in copper conductor requirements, heat generation, and transmission losses. Per bank research and industry analyst firms, 800VDC architecture delivers an end-to-end efficiency improvement of roughly 5-10% versus traditional architectures and can transmit approximately 150% more power through the same copper infrastructure compared to 415V AC, translating to approximately 45% less copper required overall.
>

> 2. Anatomy of a Microturbine: How the System Works
>
> A Capstone microturbine operates on the same thermodynamic principle as a jet engine — the Brayton cycle — but instead of producing thrust, it generates electricity and recoverable thermal energy. The entire system is built around a single rotating shaft that carries three critical components: the compressor, the turbine wheel, and the permanent magnet generator. This single-shaft, single-moving-part architecture is the foundation of Capstone's reliability and total cost of ownership advantage.
>
> The operating cycle proceeds as follows: ambient air is first drawn across the generator housing, cooling it passively. The air then enters a single-stage centrifugal compressor, which pressurizes it. The compressed air flows through the recuperator — a compact annular heat exchanger — where it absorbs thermal energy from the exhaust stream, preheating the combustion air and substantially improving thermal efficiency. The preheated, compressed air then enters the combustor, where it is mixed with fuel and ignited in a continuous, lean-premix combustion process. The resulting high-temperature exhaust gases expand through the turbine wheel, driving the rotation of the central shaft at speeds up to 96,000 RPM (for the C65) or approximately 60,000 RPM (for the C200 platform). The spinning permanent magnet generator produces high-frequency AC power, which is then rectified to DC and inverted back to grid-quality 50/60 Hz AC by the onboard digital power electronics.
>
> This entire process occurs with zero oil, zero coolant, zero gearboxes, and zero switchgear— a clean, sealed system where the only moving component is the turbogenerator shaft riding on a cushion of pressurized air.
