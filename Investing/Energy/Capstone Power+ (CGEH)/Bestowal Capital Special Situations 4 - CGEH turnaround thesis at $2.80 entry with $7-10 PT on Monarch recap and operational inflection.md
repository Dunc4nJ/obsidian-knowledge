# Bestowal Capital Special Situations 4 - CGEH turnaround thesis at $2.80 entry with $7-10 PT on Monarch recap and operational inflection

## Key Takeaways

- **Conservative bull thesis with explicit price discipline**: Bestowal frames [[Capstone Power+ (CGEH)]] as a special-situations turnaround with a defined entry ($2.80) and a $7-10 price target — a 2.5-3.5x return profile, not the 14x that Longview projects. The target is anchored in a DCF (WACC 14.17%, TGR 3%) plus comps to TGEN, GNRC, AMSC, [[Bloom Energy (BE)]], and CAT, putting CGEH's valuation in the same conversation as profitable industrial-power peers rather than hyper-growth fuel-cell stories. This is the more sober framing — "did the unit economics actually fix?" rather than "this is the next Bloom."

- **The turnaround is operational, not narrative**: The author identifies four specific mechanics driving the inflection — (1) management stopped selling turbines at negative margins to chase volume, lifting product gross margin from ~10% to 32%; (2) DFMA (Design for Manufacturing and Assembly) re-engineering, including a single valve assembly going from 8 parts at $626 to 3 parts at $42 (93% cost cut), which management says fully offsets recent China tariffs; (3) EaaS pivot now at 98% rental fleet utilization, converting one-time capex sales into recurring high-margin revenue; (4) August 2025 Cal Microturbine acquisition vertically integrates the largest US Western distributor, capturing the previously-shared retail markup.

- **Cap-structure analysis modeled correctly**: Bestowal walks the post-emergence stack carefully — Goldman holds 10.4M Preferred Units in OpCo (not PublicCo) representing 37.5% economic interest, structured this way specifically to (a) preserve CGEH's NOLs by avoiding a >50% Section 382 ownership change, (b) keep Goldman's stake non-dilutable, and (c) enable easier exit liquidity than 100% ownership would. Net debt at Q2 FY26 is ~$10.5M after applying the November 2025 PIPE proceeds ($33.2M debt - $7.7M cash - $15M PIPE). The PIPE was placed at $2.00 (8% premium to the $1.85 market price at the time) — the bullish signal here is the premium pricing AND board/management personal participation aligning their interests.

- **The 800 VDC + Microgrids 4 AI partnership is the AI-data-center entry**: CGEH's microturbines natively generate DC power, bypassing the AC->DC->AC->DC conversion losses in standard data center power architecture. Partnering with Ken Kajikawa's Microgrids 4 AI to package these as "AI Power Blocks" sized for Blackwell/Rubin-class GPU racks is the central revenue-doubling thesis. A single 20MW deployment would generate $20-30M revenue (20-25% of current annual). Bestowal models 5MW YoY rental fleet expansion through 2030 funded by the PIPE, but the upside case is fraction-of-GW capture.

- **Why CGEH actually failed pre-bankruptcy is instructive for the pattern**: The author frames the prior failure not as bad technology but as "treating a hardware business like a software business" — Goldman's $51M loan at LIBOR+8.75% (~13-14% by 2023) carried a covenant forcing 6MW of rental fleet build within 9 months, which combined with 300-day inventory days, 150-day stretched payables, and selling turbines at negative margins to "buy revenue" produced the September 2023 Chapter 11. This diagnosis is what makes the post-emergence margin discipline credible — the failure mode is now identified and structurally fixed.

> CGEH's revenue is broken down into three different categories reported under a single segment (microturbine systems and their related parts, rentals and services).
>
> The majority of their revenue comes from Product and accessories.
>
> CGEH designs, manufactures, and services microturbines. Unlike massive power plants that send electricity over miles of wires (where energy is lost), Capstone's machines sit right next to the factory, hospital, or data centre that needs the power. This is called Distributed Generation.

> The key differentiator here is their patented Air bearing technology. Normal engines (like a car or a diesel generator) use oil and coolant to keep moving parts from melting. Oil gets dirty, leaks, and requires constant changing (maintenance).
>
> CGEH's turbine shaft floats on a cushion of air (Air Bearings) while spinning at 96,000 RPM. A diesel generator needs maintenance every few weeks. A CGEH turbine can run for nearly a year without being touched.

> Perhaps their most exciting product is the newly announced 800 VDC system.
>
> To understand the value of this system, you have to look at how a data centre is normally powered.
>
> Electricity comes in from the utility grid as Alternating Current (AC). Firstly, it gets converted to Direct Current (DC) to charge the UPS batteries (which is effectively the backup).
>
> Next, it gets converted back to AC to travel to the server racks, and finally the power supply inside the server gets converted back to DC to power the actual chips (GPUs/CPUs).
>
> So how does the 800 VDC system help? It generates DC power natively. This allows them to bypass the middle steps, sending power directly to the server racks.
>
> By removing those conversion steps, the 800 VDC system offers extensive financial benefits to a data centre operator such as less fuel costs, space savings, etc.
>
> CGEH have partnered with a company called Microgrids 4 AI to package these turbines into AI Power Blocks.
>

> ### What went wrong post-bankruptcy?
>
> Now that we have an understanding of the business model, let's try to understand why CGEH failed.
>
> CGEH filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy on 28 September 2023. CGEH had really good technology and a sound business model.
>
> They didn't fail because the technology was bad. They failed because they treated a hardware business like a software business. In essence, prioritizing growth at all costs while ignoring negative unit economics.
>
> In 2019/2020, Capstone took on a massive high-interest note amounting to roughly $51 million from Goldman Sachs and Broad Street Credit (GS Speciality Lending Group).
>
> The **$51 million debt** was not a single loan taken out all at once for one bad project. It was a "financial trap" that the company built for itself over two years in an attempt to pivot its business model.
>
> Here is the breakdown of exactly what that money was for, who gave it to them, and why it became the poison pill that forced bankruptcy.
>
> The $51 million total was built in two main stages. $30 million was taken out in February 2019 to refinance old, smaller debt. They used this money to pay off a $15 million credit line with Bridge Bank (which had refinanced an earlier Wells Fargo facility).
>
> The remaining $21 million was taken out in October 2020. This was used to fund operations of the rental fleet for their EaaS business.
>
> Interestingly, this $21 million loan came with a specific performance requirement / covenant.
>
> Goldman effectively said: **"We will lend you this $20 million, but you MUST use it to build and deploy 6 MWs of new rental turbines within 9 months."**
>
> This forced CGEH to build machines for their own rental fleet even when it might not have been the most profitable move, just to satisfy the lender's rules.

*Form 8-K extract (5 October 2020)*
![[bestowal-cgeh-008.png]]

> For a company that historically was losing money, the terms of this debt were extremely dangerous.
>
> Interest was at **LIBOR + 8.75%,** in 2020 (when rates were near zero), this was expensive (~10%). By 2023, when the Fed raised rates, this debt was costing them nearly **13–14% interest**. They were paying ~$6–7 million a year just in interest payments. The entire $51 million was due in October 2023.
>
> Before bankruptcy, CEGH had never made an annual profit in its 20+ year history. They were losing money on every turbine they sold for years.
>
> Why? They were trying to buy revenue. They would discount their turbines to win customers, hoping that selling more units would eventually cover their fixed factory costs. It never did.
>
> They were essentially trying to make use of operating leverage which they failed to do.
>
> Even when they sold machines, they were terrible at managing the cash cycle. Two numbers from their bankruptcy filings expose this incompetence:
>
> They were holding parts and finished turbines for nearly **300 days** before selling them. That is millions of dollars of cash sitting on a shelves.
>
> Because they had no cash, they stopped paying their suppliers on time, stretching payments to **150 days**. So, suppliers got angry. They stopped shipping parts or demanded cash upfront, which froze CGEH's factory line.
>
> Let's talk a bit more about the $51 million loan from Goldman. Where did the money actually go?
>
> If you look at the cash flow statements from 2019–2022, the $51 million went into solid assets. But, these assets did not generate cash fast enough.

*Cashflow summary from 2019 to 2022*
![[bestowal-cgeh-009.png]]

> They spent millions building turbines to sit on trucks and rent out. While this is a good long-term business, it burns massive cash upfront.
>
> Furthermore, as mentioned earlier, they had ~$25–30 million tied up in parts sitting on shelves because their supply chain was inefficient.
>
> I think management at CGEH missed a trick here. In my opinion, if the original maturity was negotiated to be for a period of 6/7 years rather than 3 years, we might've seen a different CGEH today.
>
> To summarise, they simply used the debt to keep the lights on while selling products at negative margins.
>
> Management bet that if they borrowed money to build a massive rental fleet, the recurring revenue would grow fast enough to pay off the loan in 3 years.
>
> The bet failed because COVID happened, which slowed down installations. Crazy high inflation made building the turbines more expensive and interest rate spikes making debt to expensive to service.

> ### Restructuring
>
> Moving on, let's try and understand how the restructuring was actually done. This was a pre-packaged Chapter 11 bankruptcy filling.
>
> CGEH emerged from bankruptcy on 7 December 2023 70 days after filing on 28 September 2023.
>
> The restructuring was not your average one where equity holders get wiped out.
>
> In CGEH's case, equity holders retained 100% of the shares of PublicCo (subject to 37.5% dilution). See structure below.

*New Structure from Company Presentation*
![[bestowal-cgeh-010.png]]

> So how was the restructuring actually done? Let's take a step back.
>
> At petition date, CGEH owed to $53 million in principal and ~$4 million in accrued interest and fees, totalling to about $57 million.
>
> How much cash did they have? nada
>
> The first part of the restructuring was through a $30 million DIP facility offered by Goldman.
>
> This was split into two. $12 million new money DIP notes and $18 million of a roll-up of a portion of secured obligations under the existing pre-petition debt.
>
> The $12 million proceeds in new money notes were used to pay lawyers, payroll, rent, etc.
>
> Let's do some maths here, the portion of the old debt remaining is $57 million - $18 million = $39 million.
>
> Then comes the Exit facility. The DIP Facility of $30 million was converted into an Exit Note Purchase Agreement for an aggregate principal amount of $28 million, consisting of $7 million new money notes and a roll-up of a $20 million portion of the secured DIP Claim plus accrued and unpaid interest.
>
> So, out of the $30 million DIP facility, only $20 million was rolled up leaving Goldman with an outstanding balance of $10 million.
>
> Now, $39 million of old debt remaining + $10 million of DIP financing remaining gives a total of $49 million to Goldman. This $49 million was converted to 100% of preference shares in the OpCo convertible into 37.5% stake in the restructured business i.e. common stock.
>
> Doing the math, this means that Goldman valued CGEH at $49 million * 100% / 37.5% = $130.67 million.
>
> At the time of bankruptcy, the company was worth far less. So, Goldman took a significant realized loss on the old debt to prevent a total loss.
>
> Per the SEC fillings, Goldman's stake was valued at $13.9 million, meaning CGEH at the time was valued at $13.9 million * 100% / 37.5% = $37.1 million.

> So, why restructure like this? Goldman was the fulcrum, they had every right to take 100% ownership of the entity.
>
> For one, Goldman's stake is in the OpCo, and not the PublicCo, this means that their ownership os non-dilutable.
>
> Second, it allows for an easier exit and liquidity. If Goldman were to take 100% of the company, they would likely have to take them private.
>
> And the last thing I see is that they wanted to take advantage of CGEH's historical losses as tax credits.
>
> Per the IRS, If ownership changes by more than 50%, you lose the NOLs.
>
> By keeping the Public Shareholders as the majority owner (62.5%) of the OpCo, they technically avoided a "Change of Control," preserving the tax breaks. Goldman gets the benefit of a tax-free company without triggering the IRS penalty. Brilliant!
>
> CGEH had ~$8.0 million of debt (specifically the New Money Note" from the bankruptcy exit) maturing on 7 December 2025.
>
> This was paid off by raising $15 million via a Private Investment in Public Equity (PIPE).
>
> They sold 7.5 million shares at $2.00 per share priced at a 8% premium to the market price at the time ($1.85), which is a bullish signal. Usually, distressed companies have to sell stock at a discount.

>

>
> I'll bring one of the snips above down here to give you guys a reminder of their gross margin profile.

*Total revenue Margin Profile*

> Gross Margin has improved immensely, what is the biggest driver? They stopped selling their microgrids and negative margins.

*Product and accessories Gross Margin Profile*
![[bestowal-cgeh-003.png]]

> Sales teams were incentivized on volume. To hit quarterly targets, they would offer massive discounts to close deals.
>
> Essentially, they were selling $10 bills for $9 just to show "growth."
>
> New management came in and raised prices across the board. Gross Margin exploded from ~10% pre-bankruptcy to 32% today.
>
> They also implemented a rigorous engineering process called DFMA (Design for Manufacturing and Assembly).
>
> This just means that they redesigned the machine so it needs fewer parts to build.
>
> For example, A specific valve assembly used 8 different parts and cost $626 to build. They redesigned it to use just 3 parts with the new cost being $42. _(source:[ investing.com](https://www.investing.com/news/company-news/capstone-green-energy-q3-fy25-slides-revenue-rebounds-38-as-recovery-takes-hold-93CH-4114201))_
>
> This is a 93% cost reduction on a single component. Multiply this across hundreds of parts in a turbine, and you see how the unit economics were fixed.
>
> Management explicitly stated that DFMA savings completely offset the recent tariff increases on Chinese components, protecting their profit.

*Q2 2026 Investor Presentation*
![[bestowal-cgeh-014.png]]

> They are also pivoting aggressively to EaaS. Instead of selling a turbine once, they rent it out. The rental fleet is now running at 98% utilization (sold out), generating high-margin, recurring monthly cash.
>
> Additionally, on 31 August 2025, CGEH acquired Cal Microturbine (their largest distributor in the Western US) from $10 million ($6 million cash upfront + $4 million) paid over 2 years.
>
> Previously, CGEH sold a turbine to Cal Micro at a wholesale price (lower margin), and Cal Micro sold it to the customer at retail price (keeping the markup). By buying them, CGEH now keeps 100% of the margin.
>
> In the very first partial quarter i.e. Q2 FY2026, this acquisition added $0.7 million in revenue and $0.2 million in earnings.
>
> The equation to their profitability can be described as follows:
>
> ```
> Higher Prices + Lower Production Costs (DFMA) + Recurring Rental Income = $$$
