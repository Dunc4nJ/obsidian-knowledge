## Key Takeaways

The product is a compact, beginner-friendly mini pottery wheel kit (wheel + tools, with bundle options adding air-dry clay and a paint set) that lets people make small pottery projects at home without a studio. Demand clusters around five core personas that each need different proof and different emotional payoffs: the calm-seeking beginner adult (wants "I can do this" + "this feels good"), the budget-constrained aspiring potter (wants "it's real" and "it works"), the parent buying screen-free creative time, the gift buyer (wants completeness and zero extra shopping), and the couple/friends-night cozy activity seeker. This persona mapping is what makes the general-purpose content system in [[content systems beat content calendars when assets are modular tagged and agent-operable]] actionable for a specific product, with competitive research methods from [[meta ad library research finds winners through longevity signals creative families and angle extraction]] informing angle selection — each persona requires different hooks, different proof beats, and different emotional payoffs.

Six psychological jobs-to-be-done drive all content decisions: regulate stress through tactile focus (a 2016 study found cortisol dropped significantly after 45 minutes of art-making), lower the "I'll fail" barrier (learning-curve anxiety is a top objection), make mess psychologically safe (mess/cleanup is the #1 hesitation), justify "realness" and quality (skeptics worry mini wheels are flimsy toys), make the output feel worth it (people want displayable results matching Table Clay's "Made by Hand, Made with Care" brand promise), and set correct expectations about air-dry clay durability (not waterproof or food-safe even when sealed). These jobs translate directly into the proof types taxonomy from the [[advertising angles are testable hypotheses not copywriting|angle framework]] — each job maps to a specific proof beat that must appear on screen.

The content principles are decision rules, not generic best practices: show the spin immediately in the first second (wheel moving + clay contact sells the category magic), always pair delight with anti-objection proof (any ASMR creative needs at least one proof beat — wipe-down, splash pan, clay amount, or finished reveal), keep outcomes mini and lovable ("mini" is a design language, not a limitation), normalize the learning curve then sell the progress arc (first wobble to tiny win to improvement montage), make "all-in-one" visual not verbal (bundle value converts when laid out: wheel + tools + clay + paint set with counts), treat gifting as a moment not a claim (wrapped box to reaction to first try to shelf reveal), and be honest about air-dry clay (never imply kiln durability or food safety).

Eighteen advertising angles are fully developed with 5 hooks each, proof types, and suggested visuals: Fifteen-minute clay reset, Pottery without the $300 class, Not a toy it actually works, Mess managed, First project in one sitting, Day 1 vs day 7 progress, Oddly satisfying mini pottery, Cozy coffee-table studio, Parents meet the screen-free magnet, Grandparent gift instant bonding, Date night at home, Gift that's truly complete, Tools and details matter, Air-dry clay paint and display, Make mini decor you actually use, Studio kit equals together time, The mini scale truth, From stressed to focused, and Risk reversal and confidence. The UGC system research defines seven creator brief categories mapped to the highest-friction objections and highest-emotion motivations: testimonial and review, first try and beginner journey, unboxing and setup, mistakes and fixes credibility, couples and friends night, parent and kid activity, and gift reaction. Each includes a creator prompt, shot list, "must show" requirements, and "what not to do" guardrails. Fifteen ready-to-copy creator prompts are provided as direct briefs. This is the operational content that feeds into the Proof Librarian Agent and Content Creators layer of the production pipeline.

The shared building blocks section is product-specific: visual atoms (hands-and-wheel b-roll, mess and cleanup b-roll, bundle comprehension b-roll, finished pieces b-roll), proof and trust atoms (review snippets by persona type, durability disclaimers as trust builders, "30-day money back" risk reversal), copy atoms (hook starters, caption starters, CTA bank, objection-handling microcopy for mess/learning curve/mini scale/durability). The universal tags are upgraded with pottery-specific dimensions: persona tags (Calm Beginner, Aspiring Potter, Parent, Gift Buyer, Couple/Friends), job tags (De-stress, Learn a skill, Bond, Gift delight, Make decor), objection tags (Mess, Learning curve, Wheel power, "Toy" skepticism, Air-dry durability, Mini scale), and craft stage tags (Unbox, Setup, Throw, Trim, Dry, Paint, Seal, Reveal).

The measurement section defines success signals by content intent: reach content succeeds when comments say "where do I get this?" (if only "satisfying" with no product intent, add a clearer product reveal earlier), trust content succeeds when "is it messy?" questions decrease (if that's the #1 comment, mess-proof modules need strengthening), conversion content succeeds when "what's included?" confusion drops (if persistent, kit layout module isn't clear enough). This connects to the [[meta ads strategy]] insight that CPM reflects ad quality and that improving viewer experience improves distribution efficiency.

The guardrails section defines six explicit "don'ts": don't imply air-dry clay is food-safe or waterproof, don't overpromise "no mess" (say "mess-managed" then show proof), don't show full-size pottery expectations, don't hide the learning curve behind perfection, don't make feature-dump ads with no sensory payoff, and don't build content that looks like a toy commercial (the brand promise is "Made by Hand, Made with Care" — warm, crafted tone, not plastic or juvenile energy).

> **Highest-leverage content principles for this product**
> These are decision rules -- not generic best practices -- anchored to the dossier's friction themes and what Table Clay is actually selling.
> - Show the spin immediately: If the first second doesn't show the wheel moving + clay contact, you're not selling the category magic. The product page itself positions the wheel as making pottery "feel real...at home."

> - Make "all-in-one" visual, not verbal: Bundle value converts when people see it laid out: wheel + tools + clay + paint set with counts. Table Clay's product page gives crisp bundle compositions (including clay weights and paint set contents).
> - Treat gifting as a "moment," not a claim: Gift content must show: wrapped box -> reaction -> immediate first try -> shelf reveal -> text overlay "best gift this year." The dossier identifies gifting as a major use case and a high-performing hook category for these kits.
> - Build trust by sounding like a maker, not a marketer: Table Clay's brand voice leans "handcrafted, made with care" and "studio to table." Content should borrow that tone: practical, gentle, makerly.
> - Be honest about air-dry clay: Never imply kiln durability or food safety. Instead, teach: how to dry, paint, and seal; what it's best for; what to avoid.
> - Design content as reusable modules: The provided "Total Content Library" diagram is correct to structure work as Organic + Paid + Shared Building Blocks; the highest-leverage move is to build assets once and re-cut into multiple purposes.

> **UGC system research**
> This section defines UGC categories that are especially relevant to Table Clay because they map directly to the highest-friction objections (mess, quality, learning curve, air-dry durability) and the highest-emotion motivations (calm, bonding, gifting delight).
>
> Testimonial and review UGC:
> Creator prompt: "Talk to camera: who you are, what you expected, what surprised you, what you made."
> Shot list: 1) Creator selfie: "I thought it would be a toy..." 2) Unbox + show tools 3) Wheel spinning close-up 4) Hands applying pressure (torque proof) 5) Show first finished piece 6) Show splash pan / cleanup 7) End on shelf display.
> Must show: "It works" (hands + spinning clay), "It's complete" (tools + optional clay/paint bundle), "It's manageable" (cleanup beat).
> What not to do: Don't skip the demo and only talk, Don't oversell perfection, Don't imply food-safe use.
>
> First try and beginner journey:
> Creator prompt: "Record your first attempt, then narrate what you learned in 3 beginner tips."
> Shot list: 1) "Day 1" title card 2) Clay wobble moment (honesty) 3) One fix tip (less clay, more water, elbows in) 4) Tiny win reveal 5) "Day 7" montage.
> Must show: Imperfect start becomes progress, Mini scale expectation (small clay amount).
> What not to do: Don't hide the learning curve (it makes paid creative feel fake).
>
> Unboxing and setup:
> Creator prompt: "Unbox like a gift guide: show what's included, what's optional, and how fast you can start."
> Shot list: 1) Box open 2) Flat lay 3) Plug-in + wheel spin 4) Clay unwrap 5) First touch of clay to wheel 6) Paint set reveal if included.
> Must show: Completeness: tools + bundle components, "Start right away" feeling.
> What not to do: Don't spend 20 seconds reading labels; show with hands.

> Mistakes and fixes credibility UGC:
> Creator prompt: "Show one mistake you made and how you fixed it in under 20 seconds."

> **Measurement language and guardrails**
> Measurement language by content intent:
> Reach content: Content designed to earn new attention from people who don't know Table Clay. Success = high watch-through on the first 1-3 seconds; shares; saves (for utility). If comments say "where do I get this?" -> clarity is working. If comments only say "satisfying" but no product intent -> add a clearer product reveal earlier.
> Trust content: Content designed to answer "will I regret this?" Success = thoughtful comments/questions, profile taps, DMs, link clicks after repeat exposure. If the #1 comment is "is it messy?" -> you need stronger visible mess-proof modules. If people ask "is this food safe?" -> your content is implying the wrong use-case; add explicit disclaimers.
> Conversion content: Content designed to drive purchase decisions now (offer clarity + proof). Success = high-quality click traffic + lower confusion in comments. If people ask "what's included?" -> your kit layout module isn't clear enough. If people say "this seems expensive" -> add class/large wheel cost context and show bundle completeness.
> Paid-specific: CPM = Cost per 1,000 impressions (spend / impressions x 1,000). Meta ad delivery outcomes are influenced by bid, estimated action rate, and ad quality. If you improve the experience of watching, you can improve "ad quality" signals and potentially distribution efficiency.
>
> **What we should not make**
> These are guardrails designed to prevent "high-engagement, low-trust" content that will amplify the dossier's known objections.
> - Don't imply air-dry clay is food-safe or waterproof: Crayola explicitly warns "Do not allow finished pieces to come into contact with food or liquids," and also notes sealing won't waterproof air-dry clay. Why it's wrong: it creates returns, disappointment, and comment-section trust collapse.
> - Don't overpromise "no mess": The dossier is clear that mess/cleanup fear is the dominant hesitation; calling it "mess-free" invites backlash when reality appears. Better guardrail: say "mess-managed," then show proof.
> - Don't show full-size pottery expectations: If content frames outcomes like full-size bowls/mugs, you create mismatch. Even Table Clay reviews highlight the mini learning curve and tiny-throwing reality.
> - Don't hide the learning curve behind perfection: Perfect, overly staged results make beginners feel excluded and trigger "fake UGC" skepticism. The dossier positions learning curve anxiety as a key friction point; normalize it.
> - Don't make "feature dump" ads with no sensory payoff: Mini pottery is sold visually (spin, texture, reveal). If you lead with specs, you lose the category magic.
> - Don't build content that looks like a toy commercial: The brand promise is "Made by Hand, Made with Care." The kit should inherit the same warm, crafted tone -- not plastic, loud, or juvenile energy.
