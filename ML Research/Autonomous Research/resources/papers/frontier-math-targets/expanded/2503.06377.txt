   The main focus of this paper is on sets of equiangular lines in dimension 18, which is the smallest
dimension where N (d) remains unknown. From the relative bound [16], it follows that N (18) =
N1/5 (18) ≤ 61. Greaves et al. [11] further refined this bound, proving that N (18) ≤ 59. Also,
Taylor remarked that N (18) ≥ 48 [22, p. 124]. Szőllösi [20] improved the lower bound to N (18) ≥
54. Subsequently, Lin and Yu [17] proved N (18) ≥ 56, and show that no extra line can be added
to either of the two sets of equiangular lines in [17, 20]. Their approach to constructing large
sets of equiangular lines involved removing specific lines from a set of 276 equiangular lines in
dimension 23, which is known to be unique [9, Theorem A]. More recently, Greaves et al. [13]
proved that N (18) ≥ 57 by searching for vectors in Z18 of norm 10 with pairwise inner product
±2. They explicitly provided four Seidel matrices corresponding to four sets of 57 equiangular
lines in dimension 18 and claimed to have identified numerous others. They then posed the question
of whether their four sets of equiangular lines are contained in the set of 276 equiangular lines in
dimension 23. Yoshino [24] resolved this question in the negative, and moreover proved that the
four sets are strongly maximal.
   In this paper, we show that there are a large number of strongly maximal sets of 57 equiangular
lines in dimension 18 which are realized by sets of norm 3 vectors in a certain overlattice Λ of
the root lattice A9 ⊕ A9 ⊕ A1 . We note the lattice Λ in Theorem 1.1 is chosen in such a way
that it realizes four sets of 57 equiangular lines in [13]. In fact, we verify in Appendix that the
sets of equiangular lines we found include those explicitly given in [13]. Our main theorem is as
follows. The definition of affine equiangular sets will be given in Definition 2.1. It makes possible
to represent a set of equiangular lines with common angle arccos(1/5) in terms of a set of norm 3
vectors in an integral lattice.
Theorem 1.1. There are at least 246896 affine equiangular sets of cardinality 57 with norm 3 with
respect to (0, 0, (1, −1)) in the lattice Λ up to isometry. Furthermore, all of these affine equiangular
sets are strongly maximal.
  Since affine equiangular sets essentially correspond to sets of equiangular lines, as will be intro-
duced and explained in Section 2, this theorem immediately implies the following corollary.
Corollary 1.2. There are at least 246896 sets of 57 equiangular lines in dimension 18 with common
angle arccos(1/5) up to isometry.
   However, all the sets of equiangular lines we found are strongly maximal as well, and as a result,
our result does not directly contribute to a possible of improvement the lower bound on N (18). It
may well be true that N (18) is 57.
   The main tool in our study is the concept of switching roots, introduced in [3], which significantly
simplifies the search for vectors that form sets of equiangular lines. A natural approach to searching
for vectors that form a set of equiangular lines with common angle of arccos(1/5) is to look for
vectors with norm 5 and pairwise inner products ±1 in a given integral lattice. However, enumer-
ating all such vectors with norm 5 in a given lattice is often computationally intricate, making it
difficult to directly investigate sets of equiangular lines. Furthermore, although strong maximality
can be verified by enumerating vectors of norm at most 5 in the dual of the lattice, this process is
computationally even harder. By introducing switching roots, these vectors can be translated into
an affine space along the direction of the switching root, thereby reducing the problem to searching
for vectors of norm 3 with pairwise inner products 0 or 1 in an integral lattice. Since the resulting
lattice often has a smaller discriminant, we can more easily address the problem of whether a set
of equiangular lines is strongly maximal. See Section 2 for details. In this paper, we define such
                             SETS OF EQUIANGULAR LINES IN DIMENSION 18                                  3

vectors of norm 3 as an affine equiangular set in Definition 2.1, and adopt the overlattice Λ of
A9 ⊕ A9 ⊕ A1 in Definition 3.2 as a suitable lattice.
   This paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we introduce affine equiangular sets in connec-
tion with sets of equiangular lines, and describe how to verify their strong maximality. In Section 3,
we introduce the overlattice Λ, and in Section 4, we provide a set X of vectors of norm 3 that
contains the desired affine equiangular sets. Since X is partitioned as X1 ∪ X−1 ∪ X5 , and every
affine equiangular set in X is switching equivalent to one in X1 ∪ X5 , we study maximum affine
equiangular sets in X5 and X1 in Section 5 and Section 6, respectively. In Section 7, we study
the lattices coming from maximum affine equiangular sets in X to later consider affine equiangular
sets up to isometry. In Section 8, we provide a lower bound on the number of maximum affine
equiangular sets in X up to switching, and a lower bound on the number up to isometry in Corol-
lary 8.2. In addition, we verify that the maximum affine equiangular sets in X are strongly maximal
in Theorem 8.4, and obtain Theorem 1.1. In Section 9, we provide examples sets of 57 equiangular
lines in dimension 18, whose Seidel matrices have only 6 distinct eigenvalues, one less than what
Geaves et al. found [13]. In Appendix, we verify that the sets of equiangular lines constructed in
this paper include those explicitly given in [13].

              2. S ETS OF EQUIANGULAR LINES AND AFFINE EQUIANGULAR SETS
   In this section, we introduce sets of equiangular lines and affine equiangular sets, and describe
the relations between them. In general, an isometry is defined as a mapping between metric spaces
that preserves metric. In this paper, we treat lines and vectors in a Euclidean space, and hence
isometries are compositions of translations and orthogonal transformations. Furthermore, we will
restrict our attention to isometries which fix certain points, such as the origin or a specific vector
(e.g., a switching root), and consequently preserve inner products. For simplicity, we also refer to a
function defined on a set of vectors that preserves inner products as an isometry.
   First, we introduce concepts related to sets of equiangular lines. For a set Φ of equiangular
lines, denote by ⟨Φ⟩R the smallest vector subspace containing all lines in Φ. Two sets Φ and Φ′ of
equiangular lines are said to be isometric if there exists an isometry f : ⟨Φ⟩R → ⟨Φ′ ⟩R such that
f (Φ) = Φ′ . Here, note that f (Φ) denotes {{f (u) : u ∈ l} : l ∈ Φ}. A set Φ of equiangular lines
is said to be strongly maximal if there is no set of equiangular lines properly containing Φ even if
the dimension of the underlying space is increased. Also, a Seidel matrix is a symmetric matrix
with diagonal entries 0 and off-diagonal entries ±1. Two Seidel matrices are said to be switching
equivalent if they are similar under some signed permutation matrix. For a non-negative integer
n, denote by [n] the set {1, . . . , n}. For a set Φ = {li }ni=1 of n equiangular lines with common
angle arccos(α), there exist unit vectors v1 , . . . , vn such that li = Rvi for every i ∈ [n]. Then, the
matrix (1/α)(I − G) is a Seidel matrix, where G is the Gram matrix of v1 , . . . , vn . Although vj
can be replaced with −vj as a unit vector generating lj for some j, this Seidel matrix is uniquely
determined up to switching equivalence. In particular, the characteristic polynomial of the Seidel
matrix S is an invariant of the set Φ of equiangular lines.
   Next we introduce the concept of affine equiangular sets. Note that a vector v is called a root if
(r, r) = 2.

Definition 2.5. An affine equiangular set ω with respect to a switching root r is said to be strongly
maximal if there is no affine equiangular set with respect to r properly containing ω, even if the
dimension of the underlying space is increased.

   Note that an affine equiangular set is strongly maximal if and only if the set of equiangular lines
induced by it is strongly maximal. Also, for a set S of vectors, denote by ⟨S⟩ the lattice generated
by S, that is, the set of linear combinations of vectors in S with integral coefficients. Although, in
general, an additive subgroup of Rn is called a lattice if it is discrete, all sets written as ⟨S⟩ in this
paper are discrete.
Lemma 2.6. An affine equiangular set ω with norm s with respect to r is strongly maximal if and
only if there is no vector v in the dual of the lattice ⟨ω ∪ {r}⟩ such that (v, v) ≤ s, (v, r) = 1 and
(v, u) ∈ {0, 1} for every u ∈ ω.
Proof. The affine equiangular set ω is not strongly maximal if and only if there exists a vector w
in some vector space containing ⟨ω ∪ {r}⟩ such that (w, r) = 1 and (w, u) ∈ {0, 1} for every
u ∈ ω. Let v be the orthogonal projection of such a vector w onto the vector space generated
by the vectors of ω together with r. Then, v is contained in the dual lattice of ⟨ω ∪ {r}⟩, and
satisfies (w, u) ∈ {0, 1} for all u ∈ ω and (w, r) = 1. Thus, we obtain the necessary and sufficient
condition.                                                                                        □
