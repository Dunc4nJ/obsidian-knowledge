                                                                                             Abstract
                                                    The Hessian conjecture asks whether a polynomial with nonzero constant Hessian deter-
                                                minant has a polynomial gradient inverse. It is known in dimensions at most three, false in
                                                dimensions at least five, and open in dimension four. We prove its four-variable quartic case.
arXiv:2608.14217v1 [math.AG] 14 Aug 2026




                                                    The top homogeneous part has zero Hessian determinant and, by the four-dimensional
                                                homogeneous Hesse theorem, is a cone. We divide its cone representative into three exhaustive
                                                types: a genuinely ternary quartic with nonzero ternary Hessian, a genuinely binary quartic,
                                                and a fourth power of a linear form. In the first type, the degree-seven determinant equation
                                                forces the cubic part to be affine-linear in the cone direction. In the binary type, the degree-six
                                                equation gives a constant null direction in a two-variable Hessian of the cubic part. In the
                                                unary type, the degree-five equation and a constant-direction lemma give the same conclusion.
                                                Every type therefore reduces to
                                                                   f = P (x1 , x2 , x3 ) + x4 Q(x1 , x2 , x3 ) + ax24 ,   deg Q ≤ 2.
                                                We prove, independently of the degree or top part of P , that every constant-Hessian polynomial
                                                of this form has a polynomial gradient inverse. The branch a ̸= 0 descends from the known
                                                three-dimensional Hessian conjecture after a Schur complement. When a = 0, an isotropic-
                                                cone rank analysis eliminates rank two, solves the rank-one exception by an explicit triangular
                                                inverse, and reduces rank zero to the two-dimensional Hessian conjecture. The coupled degree-
                                                six identity is retained throughout; no component with respect to a fixed quadratic form is
                                                separated.

1     Introduction
For f ∈ C[x1 , . . . , xn ], write

                              Hess(f ) = (∂i ∂j f )i,j ,       ∇f = (∂1 f, . . . , ∂n f ).

Definition 1.1 (Hessian conjecture, HCn ). If det Hess(f ) ∈ C× , then ∇f : Cn → Cn is a
polynomial automorphism.

    The conjecture is elementary for n = 1, was proved for n = 2 by Dillen [1], and was proved for
n = 3 by de Bondt [2]. It is false for n ≥ 5 [3], and is open only for n = 4. Moreover, HC4 implies
the plane Jacobian conjecture by the standard doubling construction [4]. In degree at most two
the gradient is affine, while a cubic potential has quadratic gradient and is covered by Wang’s
theorem on quadratic Keller maps [5]. Thus degree four is the first unresolved degree in dimension
four. A related boundary case was already known: Hubbers proved the Jacobian conjecture for
cubic-homogeneous Keller maps in dimension four [6]. After normalizing the invertible linear
part of the gradient, this covers potentials of the form f4 + f2 + f1 + f0 , with fi homogeneous
of degree i: if A = Hess(f2 ), then constancy of the Hessian determinant gives det A ̸= 0, and
A−1 (∇f − ∇f (0)) = x + H3 (x) with H3 cubic homogeneous. The result below allows an arbitrary
cubic part as well, and hence treats every quartic potential.

Theorem 1.2 (Main theorem). Let f ∈ C[x1 , x2 , x3 , x4 ] have degree four. If

                                                det Hess(f ) ∈ C× ,

then ∇f is a polynomial automorphism of C4 .

   The proof has two logically separate parts. First, the top homogeneous form is classified and
each possible type is shown to produce a constant direction in the cubic part. Second, a uniform
proposition in that direction proves invertibility without any nondegeneracy assumption on the
top form.

Remark 1.3 (The corrected degree-six equation). In the genuinely ternary branch, write

                       f4 = φ(x1 , x2 , x3 ),      f3 = g(x1 , x2 , x3 ) + x4 h(x1 , x2 , x3 ),

and A = Hess3 (φ). If the x24 -coefficient of f2 is a, the degree-six determinant equation is

                                       2a det A − (∇h)T adj(A)∇h = 0.

The two terms may cancel: h is a fixed polynomial, not a free parameter. The proof never separates
them and does not attempt to deduce a = 0.

2     Preliminaries
2.1   Coordinate changes and cone tops
All changes of variables below are constant invertible linear maps or source translations. If
fe = f ◦ T , then

                   ∇fe(x) = T T (∇f )(T x),        Hess(fe)(x) = T T Hess(f )(T x)T.

Thus the Hessian determinant changes only by a nonzero constant factor and gradient invertibility
is preserved. Source translations have the analogous affine-conjugacy property and do not alter
the top homogeneous part.

Lemma 2.1 (Top homogeneous Hessian). Let f = dk=0 fk be the decomposition into homoge-
                                                       P
neous parts, with d ≥ 3. If det Hess(f ) is constant, then

                                         det Hess(fd ) ≡ 0.

Proof. The entries of Hess(fk ) have degree k − 2. The homogeneous component of maximal
possible degree n(d − 2) in the determinant is obtained by taking Hess(fd ) in every entry, hence
equals det Hess(fd ). A constant determinant has no positive-degree component.

Theorem 2.2 (Homogeneous Hesse theorem in low dimension). Let h be homogeneous in 2 ≤
n ≤ 4 variables over C. If det Hess(h) = 0, then after a constant invertible linear change, h
depends on at most n − 1 variables.

Proof. This is the low-dimensional case of the classical theorem of Gordan and Noether [7]; see
de Bondt [8, Theorem 3.1] for a modern statement and proof in dimensions at most four.

Lemma 2.6 (Descent of a polynomial inverse). Let R = C[t1 , . . . , tm ], K = Frac(R), and F ∈
R[x1 , . . . , xn ]n . Assume

                              det Jx F ∈ R× = C× ,        F −1 ∈ K[y1 , . . . , yn ]n .

Then F −1 ∈ R[y1 , . . . , yn ]n .

Proof. Subtract F (0) from the target and let L = Jx F (0). Since det L ∈ C× , an affine target
change reduces to
                       Fe(x) = x + H(x),     H ∈ R[x]n ,    ordx H ≥ 2.
The unique formal inverse has the expansion
                                                          X
                                         Fe−1 (y) = y +         Gd (y),
                                                          d≥2


where Gd is homogeneous of y-degree d. Comparing total degree in Fe(Fe−1 (y)) = y recursively
expresses each Gd over R. The inverse over K is this same formal inverse and is a polynomial, so
only finitely many Gd are nonzero. Undoing the affine target changes proves the claim.

Lemma 2.7 (Transfer to characteristic-zero fields). If HCn holds over C, then it holds over every
field of characteristic zero.

Proof. Let K have characteristic zero and let f ∈ K[x1 , . . . , xn ] have nonzero constant Hessian
determinant. Choose a subfield k ⊂ K, finitely generated over Q, which contains the coefficients
of f and the inverse of that determinant. Every finitely generated characteristic-zero field embeds
in C: choose a transcendence basis over Q, send it to algebraically independent complex numbers,
and extend across the resulting finite algebraic extension. After such an embedding, HCn over C
supplies a polynomial inverse of ∇f .
    To descend it, subtract ∇f (0) from the target and normalize the invertible linear part. The
coefficients of the unique formal inverse are then determined recursively from coefficients in k,
exactly as in Lemma 2.6; hence they lie in k. The polynomial inverse over C coincides with this
formal inverse, so only finitely many of these coefficients are nonzero. It therefore belongs to
k[y]n , and hence gives an inverse over K.

3     Classification of quartic cone tops
Let f = f4 + f3 + f2 + f1 + f0 have degree four and nonzero constant Hessian determinant. Lemma
2.1 and Theorem 2.2 allow a linear change after which

                                           f4 = φ(x1 , x2 , x3 ).

There are three exhaustive cases.

 1. If det Hess3 (φ) ̸≡ 0, the top is genuinely ternary.

 2. If det Hess3 (φ) ≡ 0, Theorem 2.2 in three variables makes φ binary. If its binary Hessian is
    nonzero, the top is genuinely binary.

 3. If the binary Hessian also vanishes, Lemma 2.3 gives φ = cL4 . Since f has degree four, c ̸= 0;
    after a linear change the top is unary, f4 = cx41 .

    The next two sections show that all three cases lead to the same structural form.

7     Proof of the main theorem
Proof of Theorem 1.2. By Lemma 2.1, det Hess(f4 ) = 0. Theorem 2.2 reduces f4 to at most three
variables. The classification of Section 3 is exhaustive.
    If the cone top is genuinely ternary, Proposition 4.1 gives the common structural form. If it is
genuinely binary or unary, Proposition 5.3 gives the same form. Proposition 6.1 then proves that
∇f is a polynomial automorphism in every case.

Corollary 7.1 (Quartic HC4 ). The Hessian conjecture in dimension four holds for every potential
of degree at most four.

Proof. Degrees at most two are affine-gradient cases, degree three follows from Wang’s quadratic
Keller-map theorem, and degree four is Theorem 1.2.

8     Discussion
8.1    What remains open
Theorem 1.2 proves the complete quartic case, not the full four-dimensional Hessian conjecture.
For degree at least five, the top form is still a cone by Theorem 2.2, but the determinant contains
more homogeneous layers and the degree-seven/six and degree-six/five arguments above do not
carry over verbatim.
    Proposition 6.1, however, has no degree bound on P . Consequently it already proves arbitrary-
degree cases which admit a coordinate form

                      P (x1 , x2 , x3 ) + x4 Q(x1 , x2 , x3 ) + ax24 ,   deg Q ≤ 2.

8.2    Relation to the plane Jacobian conjecture
For a Keller map F : Cn → Cn , the potential

                                            Φ(x, y) = y T F (x)

satisfies
                det Hess(Φ) = (−1)n (det JF )2 ,            ∇Φ(x, y) = JF (x)T y, F (x) .
                                                                                       

Thus HC2n implies JCn . In particular, the full HC4 would imply the still-open JC2 , but the
quartic result here addresses only a bounded-degree part of HC4 .
