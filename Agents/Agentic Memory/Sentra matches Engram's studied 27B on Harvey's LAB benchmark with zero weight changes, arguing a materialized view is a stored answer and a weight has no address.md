## Key Takeaways

- **The headline is a null result argued with unusual discipline, and the discipline is the reason to take it seriously.** 70.7% mean criteria pass / 36.0% task all-pass against Engram's studied-27B 70.1% / 31.0%, at $0.15 vs $0.13 per query — and the author refuses the win: "We're not going to tell you that 5 points of task all-pass is a win. At this resolution that gap is plausibly noise." The claim is narrowed to one sentence, "Corpus-specific fine-tuning was not necessary to reach this quality regime in this environment," with three explicit disclaimers (not a claim to have beaten Engram, not a claim that studying doesn't work, both approaches "seem to work equally well"), and the run artifacts are public. What is actually load-bearing is the *absent* training run — 65 minutes from ingestion start to fully queryable across 9,284 files and 266 matters — and its commercial consequence, which is the sharpest line in the piece: "Every new firm on Sentra is at full quality on day one," while a study recipe needs a training run per firm and reopens the question on every material corpus update. For scale, Harvey's own frontier baselines with no firm-specific training scored 56.2–63.5% / 25.0%.

- **The vault holds the other side of this from Harvey's own announcement, which makes the comparison unusually well-grounded — but also exposes a scope subtlety.** [[Harvey's Tenet post-trains Kimi K3 with GSPO in rubric-graded legal environments, doubling LAB hold-out completions while co-optimizing cost via reward shaping|Engram appears in the vault already, as one of three capability tracks in Harvey's Tenet post]] — a Qwen3.8-27B that consolidates a ~100M-token firm corpus into ~1M tokens of structured notes and internalizes them in weights via distillation plus RL over self-generated data, at −58% tokens, −90% cost per query and 3x intelligence-per-token — alongside Tenet itself, a Kimi K3 base post-trained with GSPO to nearly 2x LAB hold-out completions. LAB now has three architectures measured on it: **RL into weights** (Tenet), **study into weights** (Engram), and **nothing in weights** (Sentra) — with [[Toast 1 takes over the search loop as a specialized subagent - 3.5x fewer tokens at identical Harvey-bench scores and OfficeQA SOTA at 1.15 dollars per task|Toast 1's specialized search subagent]] as a fourth, harness-level answer on Harvey-bench. The subtlety worth holding: Sentra matched Engram's *firm-knowledge* track, not Tenet's *long-horizon agentic capability* track. The article doesn't conflate them, but "no fine-tuning needed" reads broader than what was tested — and the vault holds direct counter-evidence in [[parametric memory encoding cross-sample reflection patterns into weights produces more diverse and effective self-improvement than retrieval|ParamMem, which finds a LoRA of internalized reflection patterns beats retrieval on diversity and self-improvement]]. Gopinath's narrow phrasing ("in this environment") is doing real work: it contests necessity on a static corpus, not the general superiority of retrieval over parameters.

- **The disagreement is located precisely, and it is a genuine architectural distinction rather than positioning: "A materialized view is a stored answer."** Engram describes both its notes and its weights as materialized views — precomputed, cached, cheap at query time. Sentra stores no answers: an **append-only ledger of fact chunks**, each carrying its source span, its time, and who may know it; current state is a **fold over that ledger maintained continuously between questions**, not rebuilt per prompt; and relationships are never stored as a graph — "the graph is compiled fresh for each question, for the specific asker, at a specific moment, and then released." This is the first empirical test of the architecture the vault captured as [[every representation is an IR - the append-only semantic ledger is memory and vectors, graphs, and context windows are views compiled from it|the append-only semantic ledger with every representation as a compiled view]], and it extends [[memory is a compiler not a database - Ashwin Gopinath argues admission and action utility functions are the moat, and silence is the evidence they work|the same author's memory-is-a-compiler thesis]] to its conclusion: "The model is the compiler, but the memory is the asset." The Karpathy framing he borrows — documents as source code, the LLM as compiler, structured knowledge as an executable — fits both systems; the difference is only where the compiled artifact lives and whether it is discarded. Note the direct tension with [[The LLM Wiki compiles a corpus into maintained markdown at ingest, but a wiki is not user memory (mem0's State of Agent Wikis)|the LLM-wiki pattern of compiling knowledge once at ingest into maintained pages]] — Sentra compiles at *ingest* on the write side but refuses to persist the compiled *view*, which is exactly what a wiki is.

- **The governance argument is the real differentiator and it is structural, not empirical — no benchmark can score it.** Because nothing about the firm is in the weights, you get per-user access control, provenance, and deletion; the compressed version is "A weight has no address." The sharpest formulation is the question he says the benchmark cannot ask: same firm, same question, two lawyers — one on the matter, one walled off from it. Should they get the same answer, and *can a model that studied the firm into its weights even tell them apart?* Two costs he argues stay invisible on a synthetic corpus and turn blocking on real client data: **rubric authoring** (a study run must measure itself against something, so each firm either reproduces Harvey's 250-task rubric exercise with associate hours scaling in corpus complexity, or scales study blind with no way to detect regression on a matter type) and **privileged-data review** (the study loop self-generates Q&A from the corpus, so on privileged material every generated example is derivative work from client-confidential content landing in weights that serve all users — expert GC review, repeated every study run). Both costs are corroborated in the vault rather than taken on faith: [[reproducing KARL requires a closed-corpus rollout stack with synthetic frontier tasks and compression-aware RL|KARL shows what a closed-corpus study stack actually takes to stand up]] — exploration rollouts, synthetic task generation, compression-aware RL — and [[Nova Escola's lesson-planner evals worked only after error analysis rewrote the rubric - annotators agreed worse than chance until experts defined good|Nova Escola found annotators agreeing worse than chance until domain experts rewrote the rubric]], which is why "reproduce Harvey's 250-task rubric exercise per firm" is not a rounding error. This is the enterprise-governance case against weight-resident knowledge, and it generalizes past law; it is the same provenance-and-deletion argument as [[a file system is not all you need - databases beat markdown for agent context provenance and governance|databases beating markdown for agent context governance]], escalated from files to parameters, and it converges with [[Glean argues enterprise indexing is necessary but not sufficient - the real unit is a unified permission-aware index inside a system of context of indexes, graphs, memory, connectors, and tools|Glean's permission-aware index]] and [[Palantir Ontology gives enterprise agents a decision-centric substrate by surfacing data logic and action as tools governed by one security model|Palantir's one-security-model substrate]]. The per-asker view is also the operational form of the per-role lenses in [[Company Brain (Ashwin Gopinath series)|his own Company Brain series]]. Read against it, [[Harrison Chase argues companies must own their intelligence by controlling the model-harness-context system its governance and the compounding feedback loop|Harrison Chase's own-your-intelligence argument]] — cited by Harvey as the frame for letting firms own their models — inverts: if governance is the reason to own your intelligence, Gopinath's claim is that governance is precisely what weights cannot provide.

- **The task-020 error analysis is the best methodological content here, and it is an argument about benchmark validity rather than about Sentra.** LAB expects matter 1005-00001 ($280M free-and-clear incremental capacity); Sentra answered 1006-00006 (an executed $130M incremental term loan amendment). But it had found both, read both agreements, identified the $280M correctly, and made the distinction — then ranked executed precedent above unused capacity, because a lawyer drafting an amendment wants a starting point, not the biggest figure. "A system that never found 1005-00001 gets a zero. A system that found both matters, read both agreements, understood both numbers, and ranked the wrong one for the rubric gets the same zero. The score might be the same, but they are not the same failure." He then declines the easy fix: "Tuning to the rubric would raise the score but make the memory worse at its actual job." He also concedes this is the mechanism behind his own headline noise — with 250 tasks, "a dozen tasks like this move the aggregate by the whole gap." The transferable point for anyone reading agent benchmarks: a single aggregate score cannot distinguish retrieval failure from ranking-under-ambiguity, and the fix that raises the number can lower the utility. It is a worked instance of [[benchmarks are measurement instruments not question collections - regulargio's first-principles guide to claims, graders, coverage, and uncertainty|treating a benchmark as a measurement instrument with stated uncertainty rather than a question collection with a leaderboard]].

- **Caveats to hold: self-run by the CEO of one of the two systems, and his own supplied evidence cuts against the "same class of model" claim on the axis that matters most.** Disclosure is present at the bottom of the article. The Artificial Analysis figure he includes shows Gemini 3.6 Flash level with Engram's Qwen3.8 27B base on Intelligence (52 / 52) and Coding (68 / 69) but **behind on Agentic, 51 vs 41** — and he flags this himself as "arguably the axis that matters most for a benchmark about searching a corpus," which is a point against his own setup that he publishes anyway. Scoring is majority-of-three-judges (strict all-three scoring drops it to 69.9% / 34.0%), so it inherits the usual LLM-as-judge variance — the vault's own [[LangChain and Harvey show DeepSeek batch verifiers reduce legal agent evaluation costs by three orders of magnitude at acceptable accuracy|Harvey/LangChain work on batch legal verifiers]] is about exactly that machinery. Read against [[Sam Z Liu's context gold rush map - why everyone is building the same org-level company brain|the company-brain market map]], this is a memory vendor establishing that the fine-tuning path is optional; Part 2 is promised on "three things a weight can't hold." The strongest non-legal case on the other side remains [[Bridgewater and Thinking Machines fine-tune Qwen3-235B to replicate expert investor judgment, beating frontier LLMs on financial information-filtering at 13.8x lower cost|Bridgewater fine-tuning Qwen3-235B on a proprietary expert corpus to beat frontier models at 13.8x lower cost]] — a professional-services firm concluding the opposite, that the training data *is* the moat.

> Article: Study Outside the Weights

> *Article cover — Part 1 of 3. The headline result table plus an illustration of the memory surface: a natural-language question resolving against fact chunks carrying task IDs, versions, and source memos.*

> > TL;DR: No training run, zero weights changed. and we still matched Engram's fine-tuned result on Harvey's law-firm benchmark, 65 minutes after ingestion started, on a Flash-class model. Their studied 27B model scored 70.1% / 31.0%; we scored 70.7% / 36.0% at the same cost per query. Call the gap noise if you want, but the training run that isn't there is the result. And, because nothing lives in the weights, the  memory does what a checkpoint can't, per-user access control, provenance, and deletion all of which are enterprise necessity.
>
> Last month @EngramLab and @Harvey published a couple of research posts worth taking quite seriously. Give an agent a law firm's entire document history and let it study the whole corpus for a while before questioning it, and it turns out this approach beats a frontier model that reads the firm cold. Their recipe compiles the firm into the model: thousands of exploration rollouts, self-generated training data, lightweight knowledge modules trained per corpus. It works. 70.1% mean criteria pass, 31.0% task all-pass, about $0.13 per query against $1.32 for the frontier baseline. The premise is right: an agent shouldn't have to rediscover the organization it's a part of every time someone asks it a question.
>
> We seeded the same benchmark data into @sentra_app's organizational memory in which nothing about this firm goes into a weight. No LoRA trained on Calderwood & Harkness, no firm-specific checkpoint or synthetic training examples touching parameters. The model that answered the questions in the benchmark was parametrically identical to the model that existed before the corpus was ingested, the only thing that changed was that we added our memory system to the model. Result: 70.7% mean criteria pass, 36.0% task all-pass, the entire ingestion done in 65 minutes, and a per-query cost of $0.15, all on Gemini 3.6 Flash. Those are majority-of-three-judges figures; strict scoring, where all three judges must pass a criterion, gives 69.9% and 34.0%. So, an external memory system, without putting anything about the firm into the weights, reached the 70% / 30% quality regime that Engram reached after corpus-specific study. For context, Harvey's high-reasoning frontier baselines, also with no C&H-specific training, scored 56.2 to 63.5% mean criteria pass and 25.0% task all-pass.
>
> *The headline comparison: Engram 70.1% / 31.0% with org-specific retraining, Sentra 70.7% / 36.0% with weights unchanged.*
> ![[ashwingop-859320-002.jpg]]
>
> ## The interesting number is the one that isn't there
>
> We're not going to tell you that 5 points of task all-pass is a win. At this resolution that gap is plausibly noise, especially since it's possible that our harnesses, caching policies, and rubric ambiguity all move a couple of points. The number that isn't there is the training run as engram's frontier point is the top of a compute-scaling curve. Sentra reached the same quality regime through ingestion: one org, 9,284 files, 266 matters, ingestion start to fully queryable corpus in 65 minutes and the first correct benchmark answers minutes later.
>
> That difference really comes to the forefront when one thinks of how one would commercialize both approaches. Every new firm on Sentra is at full quality on day one. Under the kind of study recipe Engram talks about, every new firm needs its own training run before the model is useful for that firm, and every material corpus update reopens the question of when to run it again.
>
> ## What Engram got right
>
> Read their numbers, because they're real. After study, their agent opens with a targeted search 99.2% of the time, against 3.2% for the base model. It stops wasting turns opening its own notes and it reaches the right matter in fewer steps. Closed-book knowledge of the firm, no tools and no documents, goes from 4.7% to 72.6% over about a thousand training steps. That's an agent that knows its firm the way a senior associate does, on a 27B model.
>
> It validates something important. Compression at write time works. Reading everything once, deeply, and organizing what you learn beats starting cold on every question. Sentra makes exactly the same bet on the write side, i.e. ingestion is study. So the disagreement isn't whether to study. It's what the studying is allowed to produce, and what happens the morning after.
>
> ## Where the study goes
>
> Engram describes both its notes and its weights as materialized views: precomputed views of the data, cached so they're cheap to reach at query time. That's a precise description, and it's the whole disagreement. A materialized view is a stored answer.
>
> Sentra stores no answers. It keeps a governed record of what happened: an append-only ledger of fact chunks, each carrying its source span, its time, and who may know it. Current state is a fold over that ledger, maintained continuously between questions, not rebuilt per prompt. Relationships are never stored as a graph; rather, the graph is compiled fresh for each question, for the specific asker, at a specific moment, and then released.
>
> Harvey's own analysis of why frontier agents fail on LAB is that they never build an intermediate model of what the corpus contains, so they never know when they've searched enough. But, that intermediate model is exactly what the state is, just that it doesn't have to live in weights.
>
> The framing we're building on is something @Karpathy, who happens to be one of Engram's angels, has talked about elsewhere: raw documents are source code, the LLM is the compiler, and the structured knowledge it produces can be thought of as an executable. To be fair, his framing describes both systems, the main difference being where the compiled artifact lives. Engram compiles into weights once, at training time. Sentra compiles at query time, against a living record, and discards the artifact when the question is answered.
>
> The model is the compiler, but the memory is the asset.
>
> ## What the recipe needs that ingestion doesn't
>
> It also needs to be pointed out that as written and analyzed, two costs stay invisible on the synthetic corpus and will become blocking on real client data.
>
> 1. Rubric authoring. A study run measures itself against something. LAB is 250 tasks with rubrics Harvey wrote for C&H. On a real firm's corpus you either reproduce that exercise for that firm's matters, with associate hours scaling with corpus complexity, or you scale study effort blind, with no way to detect that the model got worse on some matter type along the way. There's no rubric to author in Sentra's path because there's no training signal to grade.
>
> 2. Privileged-data review. The study loop self-generates questions and answers from the corpus to build parametric memory. On synthetic documents, nothing sensitive can leak. On real privileged material, every generated example is derivative work built from client-confidential content, and it lands in weights that then serve queries across users. Any competent general counsel will want expert review of what's being synthesized and what's being packed into the checkpoint. Human, expert, and repeated every study run. Sentra never generates that intermediate corpus. Raw content is recalled at query time, gated by the asker's access, with the citation attached.
>
> @elonmusk's rule for hardware applies to these pipelines: the best part is no part at all. And, in this vein we asked whether the training run was a requirement, deleted it, and haven't had to add it back.
>
> ## The tasks we got wrong, and why getting it wrong is probably right
>
> Once the run finished, we went through our misses one at a time to understand why the answers were wrong. A pattern showed up quickly, and task 020 is the cleanest example of it. LAB task 020 asks for the firm's largest prior incremental-facility matter. The reason is practical: a lawyer is drafting a new incremental facility amendment and wants the best prior deal to start from.
>
> The benchmark expects matter 1005-00001, whose credit agreement holds $280M of free-and-clear incremental capacity. Sentra answered 1006-00006: an executed $130M incremental term loan amendment, closed July 31, 2025. So, we got the benchmark wrong, but we didn't really miss its answer. Sentra found 1005-00001 too, put both matters in the same comparison, then identified the $280M correctly, and made the distinction: unused capacity in a credit agreement versus a loan actually executed through an amendment.
>
> This was not a search, retrieval, or factual failure. The miss happened after the right evidence was on the table and it was because of the ambiguity associated with one word, "largest". When the request is read as largest available capacity, the benchmark is right; $280M beats $130M. But, the lawyer wants a starting point for drafting an amendment, and an executed amendment is a reasonable precedent to rank above unused capacity. The best real-world answer is both, labelled: largest by capacity, 1005-00001 at $280M; closest executed precedent, 1006-00006 at $130M. LAB has to collapse that into one deterministic answer, and we suspect a model that studied both matters into its weights would too, since a weight holds one association rather than two labelled ones. LAB chooses the first. That's reasonable, and we take the miss.
>
> We're not going to fix this even though it would be easy to fix by telling the system that on this benchmark "largest" means the biggest capacity figure. But, a lawyer asking this question next month wants a deal to start from, not the biggest figure. Tuning to the rubric would raise the score but make the memory worse at its actual job. That's what makes this zero useful to dig into. A system that never found 1005-00001 gets a zero. A system that found both matters, read both agreements, understood both numbers, and ranked the wrong one for the rubric gets the same zero. The score might be the same, but they are not the same failure.
>
> It's also the mechanism behind the noise we flagged at the top: with only 250 tasks, a dozen tasks like this move the aggregate by the whole gap. But, the interesting result isn't the margin over Engram. It's that the parameters stayed fixed, the quality moved and study happened. Just that it didn't happen in the weights.
>
> ## A note on the model
>
> We didn't use a frontier model to do any of this. Sentra's memory compiler ran on Gemini 3.6 Flash. On the Artificial Analysis benchmarks it sits level with Engram's Qwen3.8 27B base model on Intelligence and Coding, and below it on Agentic, which is arguably the axis that matters most for a benchmark about searching a corpus. Same class of model on both sides. One was trained at the firm, one wasn't, yet results are clear to see.
>
> *Artificial Analysis scores for the two base models. Qwen3.8 27B (xhigh) vs Gemini 3.6 Flash (high): Intelligence 52 / 52, Coding 68 / 69, Agentic 51 / 41 — Flash is level on two axes and materially behind on the agentic axis the author concedes matters most here.*

> ## The claim, stated narrowly
>
> Corpus-specific fine-tuning was not necessary to reach this quality regime in this environment. That's the whole claim. At the same time we think it is also worth making clear what we are not claiming: we do not claim to have beaten Engram; the gap is plausibly noise. It isn't a claim that studying doesn't work either; in fact it very clearly does, and both approaches to studying the corpus seem to work equally well. The artifacts from our runs are public. [https://github.com/Dynamis-Labs/sentra-harvey-lab-benchmark-results](https://github.com/Dynamis-Labs/sentra-harvey-lab-benchmark-results)
>
> The fact that our approach works is evidence that the learnings from the study can live somewhere other than the parameters. Our efforts also raise a question this benchmark can't ask, because a frozen corpus never changes and its one agent is allowed to see all of it. Same firm, same question, two lawyers. One is on the matter. The other is walled off from it. Should they get the same answer? And can a model that studied the firm into its weights even tell them apart?
