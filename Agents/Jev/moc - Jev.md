# Jev

TypeSafe AI's **System One Models** — a model class that gives up string generation to return type-safe structured decisions with calibrated probabilities, sampled in parallel rather than autoregressively. **Jev** is the first public model (early access, September 2026). This folder holds the primary source, the evidence behind its claims, and the use cases and ecosystem tools that have grown around it.

**Placement rule:** a note goes here if its primary subject is Jev, System One Models, or something built directly on the TypeSafe API. General structured-output or calibration research belongs in Skills, Evaluation and Monitoring, or ML Research and should be cross-linked from here instead.

## Primary Source

- [[TypeSafe's Jev trades string generation for parallel-sampled typed decisions with calibrated probabilities at $0.042 per MTok - the 193x and 444x claims come from four self-built workflow evals]] — Diogo Almeida's (ex-OpenAI, TypeSafe founder) launch post for **System One Models** and the first public one, **Jev**. Unstructured state in, typed values out (Bool / Score / Choice, cardinality up to 255) each with a calibrated probability, sampled **in parallel in one pass** rather than token by token, so **70ms-500ms end-to-end**, **$0.042 per MTok in and output free**, and schema violations impossible by construction. Trained with **RLCD** (Reinforcement Learning for Calibrated Decisions) instead of RLHF/RLVR, and named after Jevons on the bet that every order-of-magnitude drop in the cost of intelligence unlocks orders of magnitude more use cases. Four use-case families (smart if-statements / AI-powered workflows, map-reduce over big data, real-time UX, verify-and-guardrail everything), a published four-stage security incident-response workflow with probability-threshold branching, and Doom (10 queries/sec ≈ $7/hour) and Wikiracing demos. **The skeptic's ledger is the note's spine**: speed, price and zero type errors are checkable; the **193.6x faster / 444.6x cheaper** headline is not, since it comes from four TypeSafe-authored workflows scored against an Astra-Fable average, the 0% type-error bar is an axiom added to the plot, the LLM error rates are biased OpenRouter traffic, and on the Pareto chart Jev sits at ~68% accuracy, tied with the cheapest OpenAI workflow runs while Sol and Opus 5 score higher at far greater cost. TypeSafe deliberately publishes no public-benchmark numbers. All seven FAQ answers and the seven charts/demo frames captured, including the 22 error-rate values transcribed from the bar chart.

## Landscape

- [[Josh Rosen's Jev in the Wild sorts week-one projects into routing, context filtering, tool gating, worker supervision, fast control loops and fuzzy data queries - deterministic only because inference was expensive]] — Josh Rosen's week-one survey of what people actually built on Jev, framed by his own quote-tweet thesis: *"we're dramatically underestimating how many decisions in normal software are only deterministic because inference used to be too expensive."* Six patterns, each with named projects: **routing** (Jev picks from a candidate set of capabilities, not just models — jev-router, agent-router, jev-codex-router, hono-jev-router), **context filtering** (meaning decided at read time — Winnow, jev-sift, fast-jev-compaction, a reranker, blink; extends his own "decide later" context-infrastructure argument), **tool gating** (Jev supplies the judgment, deterministic code keeps policy and execution — jev-guard, pi-warden, Jev Shield), **worker supervision** (his own Foreman scoring Codex drift from AGENTS.md, disclosed), **fast control loops** (Browser Use's Jev Ultrafast, jev-browser, typesafe-computer-use, jev-plays-pokemon-red), **fuzzy data queries** (pg-jev, duckdb-jev, neo4jev). Embeds two of his own earlier posts as evidence and a 6,000-like designer demo. **This note is the hub**: each pattern section links the vault's first-hand captures (LangChain middlewares, Cua, jegrep, pg-jev, the judge bench, jev-align), and its **Not Yet Captured** section lists the 24 named projects the vault lacks, grouped by pattern with URLs — the dispatch queue for this folder. A taxonomy with examples, not measurements; the thesis is strongest for fuzzy queries and routing and weakest for safety gating, where the candidate set and the policy still have to be deterministic and nothing has calibrated the probabilities the gate reads.

- [[Josh Rosen's ThruWire puts Jev at the checkpoint - prove X things happened however you like, and Jev scores the fuzzy half of that contract against the artifact not the trace]] — Josh Rosen's third Jev piece and his first **vendor post**: ThruWire, his checkpoint product, whose proposition to the agent is "*do whatever you want, however you want, as long as you can prove these X number of things happened along the way.*" A checkpoint is a durable state the work must reach, carrying artifacts, provenance, evidence and receipts, with server-side schema enforcement and attested relationships to upstream artifacts — the contract used to be absolute (artifact exists, relationship valid, evidence present), and Jev lets it "express gray areas without giving up the contract": to what degree does the evidence support the claim, does the implementation satisfy its requirement, is the extracted item really a requirement. **Jev reads the artifact, not the trace** — trace reconstruction is what he rejects — and its typed answer becomes policy, steers the agent, or is recorded as data. Framed by GPT-6 Astra's reasoning being "harder to monitor" than Sol's and by his own 440-like coinage, embedded in the article, that the job is to "**build System 1.5**: connect System 1 (Jev) to System 2 (frontier reasoning models) using really good software architecture and deterministic connective tissue" — the pattern Cua, AgentRun and the SDE cascade all instantiate. **The Granola experiment carries no numbers in the prose**: one meeting → 3 checkpoints (meeting → sections → moments), per-moment Jev observations (positive reaction? material friction? confusion?); the only figures are in an embedded screenshot from ThruWire co-founder Seth Rosen's account — **420 artifacts**, 15-moment rolling averages of positivity 0.23 / negativity 0.04 / confusion 0.06 over 13 chapters. No primitive is named (Noul/Choice/Score appear nowhere), no threshold is stated, no accuracy is measured. Architecture GIF stilled at two frames; the checkpoint-as-runtime-primitive notes (Harvey Spectre, Deep Agents runtime, Opencomputer) are linked to show what ThruWire adds: semantic verification of *work state*, not execution snapshots.

- [[TypeSafe's Advanced structure page makes instructions and criteria arbitrary JSON via EntryType's open key map - field, inspect, focus and not_for are convention, and the page quantifies nothing]] — TypeSafe's own docs page, captured as the source of truth behind employee Nathan LeClaire's "USE THE STRUCTURED CRITERIA" post (147 likes, a strings-vs-objects meme whose approved example is a Lean proof-step Choice with an `ask_astra` escape hatch — Rosen's System 1.5 as a single option). The page's whole thesis is one sentence, "System One models are trained to understand structure," and its rule table says `instructions` (all three primitives), Choice option descriptions, Score level descriptions, and Noul `criteria.true`/`criteria.false` each accept `string | object | array | null` — **`EntryType` is an open key map, so there is no schema**: `field`, `extracted_value`, `question`, `focus`, `inspect`, `not_for`, `examples`, `summary`, `signals` are conventions from the six examples, not reserved names (Annabell's `scope`/`definition`/`includes` are equally valid). All six examples verbatim: an invoice with one shared `field` object driving a Noul, a near-duplicate-candidate Choice and two Scores; a `compare` array; a department rubric with `not_for`; a product-taxonomy walk with subtrees as option values; a PR-scope Score with `summary`/`signals`; a phishing Noul. **The page quantifies nothing** — no accuracy, confidence, consistency or token figure — and the sibling primitive pages say "**Start with strings**" and add structure only on observed confusion, which contradicts the post's urgency; structured criteria run roughly 3x the question tokens (the note's own estimate). The one substantive reply asked "what are the absolute minimum required fields?" and got no answer. Read with [[jev-align]] (GEPA mutates exactly these criteria strings — this page is the schema-less schema of its edit surface) and the AgentRun, Dellaert, SDE-cascade and MotherDuck notes, which all write structured criteria in the wild.

- [[Praneeth Paikray measures Jev's calibration for the first time - ECE 0.173 and Brier 0.156 lose to a TF-IDF baseline, and GEPA nearly halves the probability error]] — Praneeth Paikray (AI Solutions Architect, Databricks) runs the first **measured calibration of Jev** in the vault, and it is the empirical anchor for every note above that consumes Jev probabilities as if calibrated. Task: sentence-level adverse-drug-event screening on the public ADE Corpus v2, Jev (a two-option Choice) vs a TF-IDF + logistic-regression baseline trained on 20,395 labelled sentences, 300-sentence test sets, all eleven figures and both notebooks captured. **Discrimination**: Jev's original prompt found almost every positive (95.1% recall, F1 68.2) but flagged many negatives. **Calibration, the headline**: Jev **Brier 0.156 / log loss 1.849 / 10-bin ECE 0.173** against the baseline's **0.102 / 0.335 / 0.052** — out of the box Jev is substantially *worse* calibrated than logistic regression, and the direction is overconfidence: `confidence = 1.0` on 150 of 300 sentences with 10 wrong, and P(ADE) exactly 1.0 on 61 sentences of which 12 were negative. **GEPA** (config: `primary_metric: brier`, 100 train / 100 validation / 300 fresh test, 700 metric calls, seed 20260919) rewrote the instructions and on a fresh test set moved Brier **0.1357 → 0.0747**, ECE 0.142 → 0.069, F1 **69.1 → 79.7**, precision 54.8 → 71.4 at the cost of recall 93.4 → 90.2 (false positives 47 → 22, false negatives 4 → 6; paired-bootstrap CI for the Brier change −0.086 to −0.037) — the F1 gain is a by-product of optimizing calibration, the opposite of [[jev-align]]'s F1 objective with no calibration metric. Before/after prompts verbatim from `seed_question.json` and `frozen_candidate.json`. **Latency**: client-observed median **14.69 s, p95 15.62 s** (GEPA run median 19.59 s) against TypeSafe's launch claim of 70–500 ms — "our client-observed measurements did not reproduce that range" — the first independent latency measurement, and it undercuts every Jev-on-the-critical-path design in this folder. Costs were tiny (505 requests, $0.009; the GEPA study 1,260 evaluations, $0.031) but the reflection model's identity and cost are recorded as unavailable — the cheap half of the adaptation ledger is measured to five decimals and the expensive half is absent. The two 300-sentence test sets differ by 0.9 F1 on the same prompt, a fifth of the claimed gain; and `analysis.json` shows both prompts hit exactly 95.24% positive retention on validation (one deferred positive of 21) before falling to 93.4% and 90.2% on test — the validation set was too small to tune the screening policy against. The GEPA revision is purely additive (six new rules, nothing deleted, 503 → 2,020 characters), and the raw call journals ship unaltered so the recovery from a partial log can be checked rather than believed. Also: "a valid answer can still disagree with the evidence or label" and the API "returned no reasoning trace".

## Alternatives to Jev

- [[Stanford's CLM-8B is an open bi-encoder System One model caching actions apart from state - 13x over Jev at 1K candidates and 3 of 38 DeepSWE tasks over random where Jev loses 1]] — Jacky Kwok, Hangoo Kang, Tarun Suresh, Jon Saad-Falcon, Marco Pavone, Christopher Ré, Azalia Mirhoseini (Stanford / Hazy Research / NVIDIA Research) release **Contrastive Language Models**: an open **bi-encoder** System One model — a frozen **Qwen3-8B** state encoder and action encoder each with a 20M-parameter projection head, trained with bidirectional **InfoNCE** so a softmax over state–action dot products *is* the answer distribution — served behind TypeSafe's own endpoint name, `POST /v1/systemone`. Data recipe: 60M Nemotron DQA pairs → 30M Gemini-2.5-Flash-Lite hard negatives → 1M agent trajectories with 40% replay (the two-stage recipe beats hard-negatives-from-the-start by 7 points); scaling laws with exponents 0.144 (compute), 0.117 (data), 0.061 (head), **0.172 (encoder, fit on three points)** and an iso-FLOP optimum of ~310 tokens per head parameter. Weights, heads, and a 1 TB precomputed pretraining set are on Hugging Face under Apache-2.0. **The Jev comparison, read off the charts**: zero-shot it *ties* on the two saturated games (5/5 each) and *loses* on the two that discriminate (BFCL v4 95.2 vs 99.2, WikiRacing 26/30 vs 30/30); latency is flat at 33–44 ms across candidate count and length while Jev climbs from ~150 to 579 ms, so "**13× at 1K candidates**" decomposes into a **3.6× floor** (36 vs 131 ms at one candidate) that is local-4090-vs-hosted-API and a 3.6→13× climb that is the cached action embeddings — the protocol, hidden in a collapsed toggle, is "a 200-token state on a single 4090 GPU with 5 trials" with Jev "accessed through the TypeSafe API", which sat inside its claimed 70–500 ms here, unlike Praneeth's 14.7 s; the apples-to-apples number is the **~97× over constrained decoding** on the same Qwen3-8B and the same GPU. As a **best-of-N verifier** (Opus 5 candidates on 38 held-out DeepSWE tasks, Fable 5 on 30 Terminal-Bench 2.1 tasks): CLM **81.6% (31/38)** vs random 73.7% (28/38) vs Jev 71.1% (27/38), oracle 89.5%; TB 87.6 vs 84.0 vs 83.1 — so "Jev fails, below pass@1" is **one task out of 38**, CLM's verifier heads are fine-tuned on **405,919 DeepSWE steps from 4,701 trajectories across 113 tasks** (75 train / 38 held-out, task-disjoint; the "8k" in the head names is an 8,192-token context, not a trajectory count) while Jev is zero-shot, and how Jev was fed trajectories longer than its 32k state limit is **never stated** — confirmed absent even with all four of the blog's collapsed toggles expanded. No calibration metric anywhere; the DeepSWE heads' dataset card calls them "PRM heads", suggesting a rebadged process reward model; the reference encoder truncates states at 2,048 tokens. One author reply ("Exactly!") to the observation that state/action disaggregation is the part builders should steal; 19 other replies, incl. the unanswered "which workload gives 9×?". A multimodal CLM-35B is promised for early October. Repo resource `[[CLM]]`.

## Adapting Jev to Your Criteria

- [[Sutro's jev-align uses GEPA to rewrite Jev's decision criteria from five labeled examples - the demo moves labeled-set ambiguity 49.6 points but full-pool certainty only 0.5]] — Seth Kimmel's (Sutro) launch of **jev-align**, with the 2:09 demo transcribed and five terminal keyframes, Richard Artoul's quote-tweet framing ("take an off the shelf model like Jev, trained only on synthetic data, and tune it to your exact decision criteria simply by manipulating the prompt and labeling a few examples"), and all 16 replies. The demo: 1,000 of 8,018 Hacker News rows, binary "is this post about AI", five labels per active-learning round chosen by ambiguity, GEPA (reflection model GPT-5.6 Luna) rewriting three JSON strings — `instructions`, `true_criteria`, `false_criteria` — against jev-1.13.0, stopping early at **50 of 300 metric calls on a perfect training score**. Labeled-set mean ambiguity fell **84.4% → 34.8%** while full-pool certainty moved **87.1% → 87.7%**, i.e. the optimizer fit the five labels hard and barely changed the population. **The skeptic's read**: this is decision-boundary prompt tuning, not probability calibration — nothing measures whether a 0.7 from Jev means 70% accuracy. No TypeSafe account replied; the sharpest unanswered question was what a "bad" example looks like.

## Building and Evaluating with Jev

- [[LangChain's Sydney Runkle puts Jev inside the agent loop as classifier middleware - ModelRouterMiddleware picks the model from the latest user message and AutoModeMiddleware reproduces closed harnesses' dangerous-action gate]] — LangChain's Sydney Runkle on where Jev sits in the agent loop: tool calling and structured outputs made LLMs integrable, but every decision still cost a model call, and Jev answers typed questions about a **state** in parallel so extra questions barely move latency. Ships the `langchain-typesafe` integration (`TypeSafeClassifier.invoke(state=…, questions={"urgent": Noul(...)})`, state can be text, structured data or LangChain messages) and two *experimental* middlewares: **`ModelRouterMiddleware`** (Jev picks a model from the latest user message against per-model `criteria`, then locks it for the run) and **`AutoModeMiddleware(tools=["bash"])`** (Jev classifies tool calls as risky and blocks before execution — the closed "dangerous action" gate in Claude Code / Codex / Cursor, reproduced for any agent). Includes Diogo Almeida's 2:56 launch video transcribed, three community builds (Browserbase + Stagehand browser agent at **$0.001 per task** with the a11y tree as state; an on-chain trading bot deciding buy/sell every 300ms block; email classification on 1,500 real emails), and 49 substantive replies. **Skeptic's read**: the 200x/400x figures are the company's, repeated; the router decides once and never re-routes when a "quick bugfix" turns into repo archaeology (a reply's point); AutoMode publishes no risk criteria or error rates; a reply separates "safe to run" from "the user asked for this"; and both middlewares consume Jev probabilities as calibrated while [[jev-align]] only tunes prompt text. Replies also surface **jevcal**, a threshold-picking tool built because "everyone picks Jev confidence thresholds by vibes".
- [[Annabell frames TypeSafe's Jev as a savant for eval verdicts and routing - three question types, 255 choices, 10 score levels, and a Noul with no confidence field]] — Annabell's practitioner read of Jev as a **savant**: cannot write a sentence, returns no reasoning, and is purpose-built for the yes/no, category and rubric verdicts we spent years forcing out of LLMs at generation prices. The reference for the three primitives' *limits* — **Choice** up to 255 options, **Score** up to 10 ordered levels with the full distribution, **Noul** with **no separate confidence field** (code that reads `.confidence` on everything breaks on binaries). Two worked examples: a three-question agent-run grader (`needs_review` / `severity` / `failure_mode`) with the full response JSON (1,840 input tokens, 27 output), and an LLM-as-judge disagreement prompt rewritten as one Noul with explicit scope and enumerated true/false criteria. **Bench**: Good Start Labs graded 6,003 rubric checks — Jev matched Fable 5.1's verdict **91.5% at $160 per million answers** vs $33,000 for Fable, $400 Luna, $1,600 Gemini 3.8 Flash; DeepSeek V4.1 Flash got 93.5% at $260. **Where it falls short**, from TypeSafe's own model-jaggedness page: cannot abstain (a forced binary picks the least-wrong answer), no rationale when a trace fails, and documented **context rot** with a 64k-vs-32k limit contradiction between TypeSafe's models page and OpenRouter. Closes with a runnable script scoring Langfuse traces with a pinned `jev-1.13.0` and a 0.7 threshold. Only two replies, both thanks.

- [[Featherless's Simple Jev reproduces Jev's API on stock open models by remapping every answer to a single-token letter and softmaxing only those logits - no classifier head, and the code stamps every answer calibrated False]] — Featherless AI's open-source reimplementation of the Jev **API**, not the model: a stock open LM (README example `google/gemma-4-26B-A4B-it`; the `-classifier` suffix names the endpoint mode) is turned into a decision engine by renaming every candidate answer to a **single-character label** (A-Z then a-x, 50 max; digits for score rubrics and noul), appending an unfinished `{"answer": "` after the chat template, prefilling the shared prefix once and reading **only the permitted labels' next-token logits**, then softmaxing over those alone. `confidence` is just the largest label probability; `score` is the expected rubric index; `noul` is a rescaled mean over nine rating digits, not yes/no tokens. **The code stamps every answer `"calibrated": False`** and the demo's `blue: 1.9e-10` is what an uncalibrated logit readout looks like. Site pages (landing, how-it-works, API docs, demos, playground, driving sim, vision lab) all captured verbatim; vision-lab results screenshot embedded. Third open reimplementation alongside [[jevlike]] (trained option-attention head) — this one needs no training at all, but it is a 26B MoE per call at Featherless's **$0.28/MTok beta price vs Jev's $0.042**, the repo has **no license file**, README says 2 RPS while the site says 4, and the open HF server cannot do the vision the hosted demo shows. Demo API: `POST simple-jev-demo-api.featherless.ai/v1/classifier`, no key, 2k context.

- [[Daniel Ch's How to master Jev prescribes a 0.85 and 0.55 confidence-gate ladder under an LLM that still writes - the decision-layer architecture is additive but every number and all three charts are TypeSafe's own]] — Daniel Ch's "full course" on Jev, read as a **secondary guide**: no original measurements, every number is TypeSafe's ($0.042/MTok, 70-500ms, 193.6x/444.6x, with his own hedge that the company says these are near the high end), and all three of his charts are TypeSafe's launch charts re-hosted (pixel-compared; the vault embeds the originals rather than duplicates). What is **additive** is the decision-layer architecture: run Jev as judge not writer (LLMs generate, Jev decides, code controls, humans catch uncertainty); state + atomic question + explicit criteria instead of chat prompting; keep the state to the object, the context and the facts that change the judgment; fan out every dimension as parallel questions and combine in code; and a **confidence ladder — above 0.85 act, 0.55-0.85 escalate to a stronger model, below 0.55 to a human** — which he flags as an example whose thresholds "need to come from tests on your own data". Five workflow archetypes: universal verifier around every expensive LLM call, support control tower, lead-qualification engine, model router, giant dataset job. **Skeptic's read**: the Pareto chart under his "strongest model" opener shows Jev at ~68% vs Sol 74% and Opus 5 73%; his worked example asks three of five questions as Nouls, which carry no confidence field, so his uniform gate cannot read them; "keep the state clean" is TypeSafe's documented context-rot workaround softened; six replies, none substantive.

- [[LangChain's Jev-as-a-Judge bench puts Jev's quality-score variance 92 to 913x below three LLM judges at $0.34 against Claude's $28.17 - five weather traces, one human oracle, provider-default sampling]] — LangChain's Daniel Shea and Seán Roche run the first published head-to-head of **Jev as a judge vs LLM-as-judge**, reached via Sydney Runkle's QT ("cheaper and more precise"). Design: **five** weather-agent traces frozen as LangSmith examples, one human reviewer as oracle, each judge scoring all five **100 times** on two signals (binary `does_pass`, continuous `quality`); Jev vs GPT-5.6 Luna, GPT-5.6 Terra, Claude Sonnet 4.6 at provider-default sampling with no seed. Results, all 13 figures transcribed: accuracy on `does_pass` **Jev 100.0 / Terra 99.8 / Luna 96.4 / Claude 80.0**; mean `quality` variance **Jev 0.0000149 vs 433x (Luna), 913x (Terra), 92x (Claude)**; cost **$0.00035/call, 0.44s, $0.34 total vs $0.39 / $2.90 / $28.17**; a 10k-traces/day extrapolation of $103.59 vs $8,434.08 over 30 days. **Skeptic's read**: effective N is 5 not 500, and Claude's 80% is a perfectly flat line that disagrees with the human on one case every time; the 92-913x range inverts the story (Terra, second most accurate, is worst on variance); accuracy is measured only on the binary and variance only on the continuous, so "accurate and precise" splices two measurements; against Luna the cost gap is 11%, the 81x headline is Claude-specific; **calibration is never measured** and the article calls TypeSafe's calibration claim "observational, not evidence"; the article's own Choice and Noul bullets are swapped. Sixteen replies, none answered by LangChain or TypeSafe — the sharpest asks whether the LLM judges got a typed schema like Jev, or whether the repeatability gap is just output format. Read against Annabell's Good Start Labs bench (6,003 checks, Jev 91.5% agreement, DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 93.5%), where the larger sample does not put Jev first.

- [[Cua keeps the candidate menu in application code so Jev only picks an ID - CUA-S1-FORMS is a 706K-parameter form specialist at 99.7 percent against hosted Jev's 83.6, and neither model sees pixels]] — Cua (open-source computer-use infra) on where a System One model fits in a GUI agent loop. The design rule: **the application mints the candidate menu** — it reads DOM or accessibility state, decides which actions are allowed, attaches values, assigns IDs — and Jev only returns a Choice over those IDs; **neither Jev nor Cua's model sees pixels** ("text-only… it also doesn't take screenshots today"), with OmniParser named only as an unbuilt fallback for screenshot-only apps. **CUA-S1-FORMS** is Cua's own **706K-parameter** form specialist that scores each form element independently on a 4-way use-value / check / click / skip choice (which sidesteps the 255-option cap structurally) and reports **99.7% against hosted Jev's 83.6%** on its form task; hand-back to the general agent happens on an *unexpected* return edge rather than a confidence threshold. The only end-to-end comparisons exist as burned-in video overlays, not in the prose: Jev **44.9s / ~$0.00108** vs Astra **294.9s / ~$1.45** on one task, and "one pass, 50ms cursor glide" vs "23 tool turns, 39.6s" on a clinic registration form (both silent screen recordings, posters embedded, mp4s linked). **Skeptic's read**: vendor post announcing its own specialist; with the app building the menu, Jev is the *selector* and the hard part (grounding, candidate generation) is upstream; cardinality is never discussed; a 706K model beating Jev on its own narrow task says more about the task than about Jev. Closest prior art is Kyle Jeong's Browserbase + Stagehand build in the Sydney Runkle note (a11y tree as state, actions as questions, $0.001/task). Four of seven replies retrieved, none from TypeSafe.

- [[Can Bölük's jegrep turns Jev into a semantic grep by scoring grep-ranked candidates with one Noul per file and a Choice for the line range - $0.004 a query, and the Gemini-lite comparison is nowhere in the repo]] — Can Bölük (author of hashline and the `omp` harness) releases **jegrep**, a Rust semantic grep over a codebase, with a 22s silent demo and one substantive self-reply explaining how to make the problem "jav-shaped". Mechanism from source: a real lexical grep ranks candidates first and keeps the top **128 files**, so Jev never sees the repository; one **Noul per filename** in batches of 64 against a shared state; the top 20 files are compressed into **384-byte sketches** scored one Noul each and cut at 0.45 down to at most **40 full passages**; each survivor gets a `relevant` Noul plus a `where` Choice over 16 line ranges that becomes a line heat-map. Demo frame: 8,177 files listed, 128 judged, 23 requests, 99.8k tokens, **$0.0042, 2.2s wall** for one query. The repo ships a real benchmark — 40 labelled queries over pinned CPython, Kubernetes, Linux and Postgres checkouts, with the strategy search run by three AI agents in worktrees — and its cascade default finds 49/49 files and 93/94 regions across 30 queries at 37.5% less cost than jegrep's previous strategy. **The tweet's "beating gemini-lite agentic-retrieval at 10x less the cost" appears nowhere in the repo**; it is an unpublished measurement and the author's own hedge is "seems to be… have to benchmark a bit more". Gold labels are positive-only, so only precision lower bounds exist. Sits in the vault's code-search cluster as a *ranker over grep candidates* (the pgr thesis that ranking, not grep speed, is the lever), and beside [[pg-jev]] as the second "Jev as a query operator" design with a different batching strategy.

- [[Varun Mathur's jevcache memoizes Jev decisions by sha256 of model, schema and redacted canonical state - the 60 to 80 percent repeat rate is asserted and dropping user_id collides two subjects]] — Varun Mathur (Hyperspace AI) launches **jevcache**, a 2 MB binary that memoizes Jev decisions by `sha256(model ⊕ schema ⊕ canonical(redact(state)))` so a repeat costs $0 in 0ms, replays deterministically in CI, and can be published as a fingerprints-and-answers bundle to a hosted commons (reads keyless, writes mint a free key; roadmap: a decentralized index over the Hyperspace P2P network and per-recall pricing so publishers can charge for a cache). Backend-agnostic: your model, TypeSafe's real `/v1/systemone`, or OpenRouter; runs as a localhost sidecar, a hosted instance behind a token, or a CLI whose `recall` exits 3 on a miss. Site, install script, README and 15 of 19 replies verbatim. **Skeptic's ledger**: the **60-80% repeat rate** that drives the whole pitch is sourced nowhere; the site's calculator hard-codes 78.4% and its static markup says $4,704/month while its own script computes **$9,408**; the $0.0004/decision is sound (LangChain measured $0.00035) but the demo transcript prints $0.000042; the GitHub repo has **no source** (README and og.png only, a launch-day commit deleted the line "source is maintained privately", no license file, "free to use" instead), yet the README cites a TypeScript core and a Dockerfile that do not exist; issue #2 reproduces that `user_id`, `order_id`, `session_id` and other id fields are dropped before hashing, so **two subjects share one cached decision**, and issue #1 reports `state_preview` writing unredacted credentials to a mode-644 ledger — both unanswered. ## Use Cases and Ecosystem

Resource notes for third-party projects built on or around Jev (in `resources/`). Listed here because the folder's purpose is Jev *and its use cases*; graduate any of them into a sibling knowledge note once explored.

- [[pg-jev]] — PostgreSQL extension (210★, PGXN `jev`) that puts a plain-language condition in a `WHERE` clause and has Jev judge every row: `jev()`, `jev_prob()`, `jev_choice()`, `jev_score()`. Packs 20 rows into one shared state with one yes/no Noul question per row to amortise the ~270-token request overhead (175 tokens/row batched vs 435 alone), streams the table in physical order with TID range scans, keeps 2 × `jev.concurrency` requests in flight, short-circuits on `LIMIT` and cheaper predicates, caches per row content. Measured: 2,000 rows ≈ 3.5s / 296k tokens / $0.012 on first run, ≈ 50ms on second; extrapolates to roughly $6-7 per million rows. The concrete instance of "inference as a database operator". Skeptic notes: pgjev.com still advertises the v0.1.0 defaults, and batching by physical order means a row's answer may depend on which neighbours shared its request.
- [[jevlike]] — Independent open-source starter model (971★, created the day after the launch post) that reproduces Jev's *interface* without its design: text context plus N text options in, one probability per option out in a single forward pass. Each option becomes a query that attends over context tokens, a shared dot product scores each option-context pair, softmax across options; byte-level encoder from scratch or a frozen HF encoder. The same head scores controller buttons from image patches for Doom and chess demos, with weak results the author flags as not competence claims (0.60 kills / −97.50 reward; chess 4W-46D-0L vs random, 0W-2D-48L vs Stockfish level 0). Its eval script computes ten-bin ECE but no calibration number is ever published, which is the one figure that would test the System One claim.
- [[jev-align]] — Sutro's open-source CLI (`jeva`) that runs GEPA over the *text* of a Jev task spec — `instructions` plus the per-shape criteria strings — scored by batch F1 against your labels, in an active-learning loop that surfaces the most ambiguous rows for you to label. Read the source, not the pitch: the decision threshold is hardcoded at 0.5, no few-shot examples enter the prompt, `metrics.py` has no ECE / log-loss / Brier, and GEPA is called with the same rows as train and validation (the 20% holdout is off by default). So "calibrated to your decision criteria" means the boundary moves to fit your labels; Jev's probabilities themselves are untouched and unmeasured. Per-round call volume is derived in the note since the README publishes no cost or runtime.
- [[simple-jev]] — the `featherless-ai/simple-jev` repo (272★, no license): `common/prompt_builder.py` remaps answers to single-token labels and verifies each is single-token-stable at the rendered boundary or raises; `HFBackend._score` does one prefill of the common prefix with `use_cache=True` and broadcasts the cache per question batch; `common/response_scoring.py` softmaxes over permitted labels, sets `confidence = p[argmax]`, `score = p @ arange(n)`, and writes `"calibrated": False` on every answer with `calibration: not_calibrated` in advanced metadata. No temperature scaling, Platt, or ECE anywhere.
- [[jegrep]] — the `can1357/jegrep` repo (Rust, MIT): grep-first candidate ranking, filename Nouls in batches of 64, 384-byte sketch cascade cut at 0.45 to 40 full passages, per-passage `relevant` Noul + `where` Choice over 16 ranges; `--json` output for agents; `benches/` holds the 40-query labelled suite and the 580-line RESULTS.md, which compares jegrep strategies only.
- [[jevcache]] — the `hyperspaceai/jevcache` distribution repo: README and og.png only, no source, no LICENSE (README says "free to use"), v0.1.0 release ships five binaries with checksums (linux-x64 is 3.15 MB against the site's "about 2 MB"); mechanism recorded as documented, not as read from code; two open issues (id-field collision, unredacted `state_preview`) are the only empirical evidence about the canonicalizer.
- [[CLM]] — the `Contrastive-LM/CLM` repo (Apache-2.0, 33★ at capture): `clm-serve` (CPU, :8700, `/v1/systemone`, hot-reloaded 20M heads, embedding cache) in front of a vLLM Qwen3-8B pooling server (GPU, :8090); `train/finetune.py` (InfoNCE + hard negatives, a leftover `--task prm` path), `evaluation/bon_eval.py` (scores each step, averages the last 12), the T-Rex example, and the playground; HF: `CLM-v0.1-8B` head (75 MB), `deepswe-clm-heads-8k` (MIT), two datasets incl. the 1,023 GB Nemotron embedding shards with no card.
